\begin{abstract}
Weakly supervised video anomaly detection (VAD) aims to localize anomalous events using only video-level labels, yet most existing approaches rely on a single visual modality and assume fixed deployment conditions. We present a dual-modal fusion framework that combines skeleton dynamics from a frozen CTR-GCN encoder with visual-language features from frozen vision-language backbones, trained with multiple instance learning (MIL) ranking loss on frozen backbone features (only the lightweight fusion head is learned). We conduct a systematic study across four visual backbones---CLIP ViT-B/16, SigLIP2 ViT-B/16, SigLIP2 SO400M, and SigLIP2 Giant---on both UCF-Crime and XD-Violence benchmarks. Our gated fusion mechanism with learned modality weighting achieves 82.5\% AUC on UCF-Crime (SigLIP2 Giant) and 78.7\% AP on XD-Violence (SigLIP2 SO400M), showing that skeleton features add a small but consistent complementary gain---meeting or exceeding the visual-only baseline across all four backbones and both datasets, strictly positive in six of the eight settings---despite their lower standalone performance. These numbers trail the field's fine-tuned and text-aligned leaders---which reach roughly 88--91\% UCF AUC---a gap that follows from our deliberately frozen, no-text design; our contribution is a reproducible, single-GPU dual-modal pipeline and a label-free robustness study rather than a new peak score. We further study test-time adaptation (TTA) under 20 synthetic corruption conditions and over three seeds. We find that entropy-minimization TTA (TENT, SAR) changes AUC by less than $0.1$ percentage points on every backbone---negligible, because with the gate and detection head frozen the LayerNorm affine updates are too small to reorder the rank-based metric---and that naive feature-statistic restoration is rank-disruptive. We instead propose a label-free, tuning-free \emph{discriminative-reliability reweighting} that detects when the visual-language stream's discriminative signal collapses and routes the gated fusion toward the corruption-robust skeleton stream, improving corrupted-AUC on all four backbones (mean $+1.2$ points, up to $+13.2$ points in the single most-degraded condition---the corruption that most collapses the visual stream).
\end{abstract}

\keywords{video anomaly detection, weakly supervised learning, multi-modal fusion, skeleton graph convolution, test-time adaptation}

\section{Introduction}

Video anomaly detection (VAD) addresses the problem of identifying unusual or dangerous events in surveillance footage---a task of growing importance as the scale of video surveillance systems expands beyond the capacity of human monitoring~\cite{sultani2018ucfcrime}. The weakly supervised formulation, which requires only video-level labels indicating the presence or absence of anomalies, has emerged as the dominant paradigm due to the prohibitive cost of obtaining frame-level temporal annotations for large-scale datasets~\cite{tian2021rtfm}.

Recent advances in vision-language models such as CLIP~\cite{radford2021clip} have introduced powerful visual representations that capture rich semantic information about scene content. Methods such as VadCLIP~\cite{wu2024vadclip} have demonstrated that frozen CLIP features can serve as effective inputs for weakly supervised anomaly detection. However, such approaches rely exclusively on appearance-based features and overlook the motion dynamics that are often central to violence-related anomalies---actions such as fighting, assault, and vehicular collisions are characterized by distinctive spatiotemporal motion patterns that static visual features may not fully capture.

Skeleton-based action recognition, exemplified by graph convolutional networks such as CTR-GCN~\cite{chen2021ctrgcn}, provides a complementary signal grounded in human body dynamics. While skeleton representations have been explored for anomaly detection in constrained settings~\cite{doshi2022skeleton}, their integration with vision-language features in a unified weakly supervised framework remains largely unexplored. Furthermore, the sensitivity of such fusion systems to the choice of visual backbone has not been systematically studied, despite the rapid proliferation of vision-language model variants including the SigLIP~\cite{zhai2023siglip} and SigLIP2~\cite{tschannen2025siglip2} families.

A separate concern for practical deployment is domain shift: models trained on curated datasets often encounter distribution changes due to weather conditions, camera degradation, compression artifacts, or lighting variations in real-world surveillance installations. Test-time adaptation (TTA) methods such as TENT~\cite{wang2021tent} and SAR~\cite{niu2023sar} address this by adapting batch normalization (BN) parameters at inference time via entropy minimization, and have shown promising results on image classification benchmarks.

However, modern vision-language backbones and lightweight fusion heads typically employ LayerNorm~\cite{ba2016layernorm} rather than BatchNorm~\cite{ioffe2015batchnorm}, raising the question of whether entropy-based TTA transfers effectively to LayerNorm-based architectures and, if not, what form of test-time adaptation can recover robustness for a frozen dual-modal VAD model---questions that, to our knowledge, have not been investigated in the VAD literature.

This paper makes three contributions:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Dual-modal fusion framework.} We propose a pipeline that combines frozen CTR-GCN skeleton features with frozen vision-language features through learned gated fusion, trained with MIL ranking loss. The gated mechanism learns per-sample modality weighting, enabling the network to leverage complementary information from skeleton dynamics and visual semantics.

    \item \textbf{Systematic four-backbone comparison.} We evaluate our framework across four visual backbones---CLIP ViT-B/16, SigLIP2 ViT-B/16, SigLIP2 SO400M, and SigLIP2 Giant---on both UCF-Crime~\cite{sultani2018ucfcrime} and XD-Violence~\cite{wu2020xdviolence}, revealing dataset-specific backbone preferences and quantifying the impact of backbone capacity on fusion effectiveness.

    \item \textbf{Test-time adaptation for frozen dual-modal VAD.} We show that entropy-minimization TTA (TENT and SAR) cannot improve rank-based VAD metrics---a structural consequence of confining adaptation to the fusion head's LayerNorm affine parameters in an otherwise-frozen rank-based detector, rather than a tuning artifact---and that naive feature-statistic restoration is rank-disruptive and backbone-dependent. We then propose \emph{discriminative-reliability reweighting}, a label-free, tuning-free test-time method that routes the gated fusion toward the corruption-robust skeleton stream when the visual-language stream's discriminative signal collapses, improving corrupted-AUC on all four backbones across 20 corruption conditions and three seeds.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Video Anomaly Detection}

The weakly supervised VAD paradigm was established by Sultani et al.~\cite{sultani2018ucfcrime}, who introduced the UCF-Crime dataset and a multiple instance learning (MIL) framework with ranking loss that encourages anomalous video snippets to receive higher anomaly scores than normal ones, reporting 75.41\% frame-level ROC-AUC on UCF-Crime with C3D features. Tian et al.~\cite{tian2021rtfm} improved upon this formulation with Robust Temporal Feature Magnitude Learning (RTFM), introducing feature magnitude-based anomaly scoring and temporal smoothness constraints that have become standard components in subsequent work, reaching 84.30\% AUC on UCF-Crime and 77.81\% Average Precision (AP) on XD-Violence with I3D features. Wu et al.~\cite{wu2020xdviolence} contributed the XD-Violence dataset, which extends the problem to multi-modal settings with audio and provides a more diverse set of violence categories; XD-Violence performance is reported as AP rather than ROC-AUC. More recently, Wu et al.~\cite{wu2024vadclip} demonstrated that frozen CLIP features can achieve competitive performance when combined with temporal modeling and a learnable text-alignment branch (88.02\% AUC on UCF-Crime, 84.51\% AP on XD-Violence), though their approach remains limited to a single visual modality. Our work builds upon the MIL ranking loss formulation of Sultani et al.\ and the feature magnitude learning of Tian et al.\ while introducing a dual-modal fusion architecture that combines skeleton dynamics with visual-language features. Unlike prior single-modal approaches, we systematically evaluate how the choice of visual backbone interacts with fusion effectiveness across two major benchmarks.

\subsection{Multi-Modal Fusion for Action Understanding}

Multi-modal approaches to video understanding have explored combinations of RGB, optical flow, audio, and skeleton modalities~\cite{carreira2017i3d}. In the anomaly detection domain, skeleton-based methods have been applied primarily in single-modal settings~\cite{doshi2022skeleton}, leveraging pose estimation models to extract body joint trajectories for anomaly scoring. Graph convolutional networks, particularly CTR-GCN~\cite{chen2021ctrgcn} with its channel-wise topology refinement, have shown strong performance in skeleton-based action recognition on benchmarks such as NTU RGB+D. However, the combination of skeleton features with vision-language representations
for weakly supervised anomaly detection has not been explored.
Our fusion approach bridges this gap by combining frozen CTR-GCN features
(trained on NTU-120 for general action recognition) with frozen vision-language
features, using a lightweight gated mechanism to learn modality weighting.
The key design choice of freezing both backbone encoders ensures computational
efficiency while isolating the fusion head as the only learned component.

\subsection{Test-Time Adaptation}

Test-time adaptation seeks to improve model robustness to distribution shift without access to source training data at deployment time. TENT~\cite{wang2021tent} adapts BatchNorm affine parameters by minimizing the entropy of model predictions on each test batch. SAR~\cite{niu2023sar} extends TENT with sharpness-aware minimization~\cite{foret2021sam} and a reliability-filtering mechanism that selectively applies gradient updates based on prediction confidence. Both methods fundamentally rely on the statistics computed by BatchNorm layers, which aggregate batch-level information that captures distribution shift. LayerNorm~\cite{ba2016layernorm}, in contrast, normalizes across features within a single sample and does not maintain running statistics, making it unclear whether entropy minimization on LN parameters can capture the same distributional signals. Beyond the BN/LN distinction, both methods sharpen the output distribution and, with the fusion gate and detection head frozen, barely perturb a \emph{rank-based} detection metric. We apply TENT and SAR to the LayerNorm parameters of our fusion head to establish this empirically, and then introduce a complementary, label-free feature-space reweighting that does improve the ranking metric.

\section{Methodology}

\begin{figure*}[t]
  \centering
  \resizebox{\textwidth}{!}{\input{figures/fig_architecture}}
  \caption{Overview of the dual-modal fusion pipeline. Two parallel \emph{frozen} streams extract complementary snippet features: RTMPose keypoints (top-2 persons) are encoded by a frozen CTR-GCN into 256-d skeleton features, while frames sampled at ${\sim}1$~FPS are embedded by a frozen vision-language backbone (CLIP ViT-B/16 or a SigLIP2 variant) and aggregated by concatenated mean+max pooling into a $2d$-dimensional (1024--3072-d) visual feature. The gated fusion module---together with the MIL head, the only trained component---projects each modality to a shared 256-d space (per-modality LayerNorm), blends them with a per-dimension learned gate, and retains both projected modalities through a residual connection followed by a third LayerNorm. The dashed annotation marks the test-time reliability reweighting that scales the visual stream by $w$ under corruption (Sec.~\ref{sec:disc}). The MIL head scores each snippet and is trained with the MIL ranking loss.}
  \label{fig:architecture}
\end{figure*}

Our framework processes surveillance videos through two parallel feature extraction streams---skeleton-based and visual-language-based---whose outputs are combined via a learned fusion mechanism and trained with MIL ranking loss (only the fusion head and classifier are learned; both backbones are frozen). Figure~\ref{fig:architecture} illustrates the complete pipeline. All backbone encoders are frozen; only the fusion head and anomaly classifier are trainable.

\subsection{Skeleton Extraction with RTMPose}

We extract 2D human pose keypoints from video frames using RTMPose-m~\cite{jiang2023rtmpose}, a real-time multi-person pose estimation model based on the top-down paradigm. The extraction pipeline proceeds in two stages: (1)~a YOLOX-m object detector localizes person bounding boxes in each frame, and (2)~RTMPose-m estimates 17 COCO-format keypoints (nose, eyes, ears, shoulders, elbows, wrists, hips, knees, ankles) for each detected person.

We retain the top-2 persons per frame ranked by mean keypoint confidence to maintain a fixed spatial dimension across all inputs. When fewer than 2 persons are detected, the remaining slots are zero-padded. The raw pixel coordinates are normalized to the $[-1, 1]$ range about the image center via the PreNormalize2D transformation, matching the input convention of the NTU-pretrained CTR-GCN encoder.

The extracted skeletons are organized as tensors of shape $(T_f, M, V, C)$ where $T_f$ is the number of frames, $M=2$ is the maximum person count, $V=17$ is the number of joints, and $C=3$ includes the $(x, y)$ coordinates and a keypoint confidence score. The extraction runs at approximately 21.6 FPS on single-person scenes (about 15 FPS with multiple persons) on a single RTX 4090 using ONNX Runtime with CUDA acceleration.

\subsection{CTR-GCN Feature Extraction}

Skeleton sequences are encoded using CTR-GCN~\cite{chen2021ctrgcn}, a graph convolutional network that refines the graph topology in a channel-wise manner. Unlike fixed-topology GCNs that assume a static skeleton graph, CTR-GCN learns channel-specific topology refinement matrices that adapt the adjacency structure for different feature channels, enabling the network to capture both local joint interactions and global body configurations.

We employ a CTR-GCN model pre-trained on NTU RGB+D 120~\cite{yan2022pyskl} for general action recognition, using it as a frozen feature extractor. The model processes four complementary input streams: joint positions (j), bone vectors (b), joint motion (jm), and bone motion (bm). Joint positions encode absolute spatial coordinates, bone vectors capture relative limb orientations, and the motion streams encode first-order temporal differences of each. Each stream produces a 256-dimensional feature vector per temporal window.

Following the PYSKL~\cite{yan2022pyskl} convention, we segment each video into non-overlapping 64-frame snippets and pool the backbone output over the person ($M$), temporal ($T_f$), and spatial ($V$) dimensions via global average pooling to produce a compact 256-d snippet representation. The four-stream features are combined by weighted averaging (joint and bone weighted 1.0, motion streams 0.5) to form the final 256-d skeleton representation. Videos shorter than 64 frames (172 videos in UCF-Crime, one in XD-Violence) are excluded from training and evaluation. Because UCF-Crime is distributed as frames pre-sampled at roughly 3~FPS (every tenth frame of its ${\sim}30$~FPS source video) while XD-Violence is decoded at its native ${\sim}24$~FPS, a 64-frame snippet spans about 20~s on UCF-Crime versus ${\sim}2.7$~s on XD-Violence; both streams use identical snippet boundaries, so the skeleton and visual features stay aligned by construction. (On UCF-Crime a single snippet thus pools skeleton motion over ${\sim}20$~s---far longer than the few-second action clips on which CTR-GCN was pre-trained---a plausible factor in the weaker UCF skeleton-only result reported below.)

\subsection{Visual-Language Feature Extraction}

We extract visual features from video frames using frozen vision-language backbones. We evaluate four models spanning two architectural families:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{CLIP ViT-B/16}~\cite{radford2021clip}: The original contrastive language-image model with a Vision Transformer (ViT-B/16) image encoder~\cite{dosovitskiy2021vit}, producing 512-d frame-level features.
    \item \textbf{SigLIP2 ViT-B/16}~\cite{tschannen2025siglip2}: A sigmoid-loss variant trained on multilingual data with improved dense prediction capabilities, producing 768-d features.
    \item \textbf{SigLIP2 SO400M}: A shape-optimized ${\sim}400$M-parameter SigLIP2 encoder (SoViT-400M), producing 1152-d features.
    \item \textbf{SigLIP2 Giant}: The largest variant in the SigLIP2 family with an optimized giant architecture, producing 1536-d features.
\end{itemize}
All four models share the Vision Transformer~\cite{dosovitskiy2021vit} architecture but differ in training objective, data scale, and embedding dimensionality. CLIP uses contrastive loss on 400M image-text pairs, while SigLIP2 employs a sigmoid-based pairwise loss and is trained on substantially larger multilingual corpora. The three SigLIP2 backbones use the open-clip checkpoints \texttt{ViT-B-16-SigLIP2-256}, \texttt{ViT-SO400M-16-SigLIP2-256}, and \texttt{ViT-gopt-16-SigLIP2-256} (all pretrained on WebLI), while CLIP ViT-B/16 uses the original OpenAI weights.

For each 64-frame snippet, we extract frame-level features at approximately 1~FPS, yielding roughly 3 frames per snippet on XD-Violence and about 20 on UCF-Crime, reflecting the dataset-dependent snippet duration described above. The frame-level features are aggregated via concatenated mean and max pooling, producing a $2d$-dimensional snippet representation (e.g., 1024-d for CLIP's 512-d features, 3072-d for SigLIP2 Giant's 1536-d features). Mean pooling captures the average semantic content of the snippet, while max pooling preserves the most salient per-dimension activations, which may correspond to the most anomalous frame within the temporal window.

\subsection{Fusion Mechanisms}

We investigate two fusion strategies for combining skeleton features $\mathbf{s} \in \mathbb{R}^{256}$ with visual features $\mathbf{v} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_v}$, where $d_v = 2d$ is the dimension of the concatenated mean--max visual representation (with $d$ the backbone embedding dimension):

\textbf{Late Fusion.} The simplest approach scores each modality independently and averages the two snippet-level anomaly scores. Each modality is passed through its own classifier head---$h_s(\cdot)$ for the skeleton stream and $h_v(\cdot)$ for the visual stream---and the resulting per-snippet scores are combined with a fixed equal weight:
\begin{equation}
    a_{\text{late}} = \tfrac{1}{2}\, h_s(\mathbf{s}) + \tfrac{1}{2}\, h_v(\mathbf{v})
\end{equation}
This serves as a baseline that treats both modalities equally with a fixed weighting and no input-dependent adaptation.

\textbf{Gated Fusion.} To enable the network to learn input-dependent modality weighting, we employ a gated fusion mechanism. Each modality is first projected to a shared 256-d space and normalized:
\begin{equation}
    \hat{\mathbf{s}} = \text{LN}_s(W_s \mathbf{s} + \mathbf{b}_s), \quad \hat{\mathbf{v}} = \text{LN}_v(W_v \mathbf{v} + \mathbf{b}_v)
\end{equation}
A gating vector $\mathbf{g} \in [0, 1]^{256}$ is computed per dimension from the concatenated projected features:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{g} = \sigma\left( W_g \left[ \hat{\mathbf{s}} \| \hat{\mathbf{v}} \right] + \mathbf{b}_g \right)
\end{equation}
The fused representation applies the gated blend together with a residual connection that retains both projected modalities, followed by dropout:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{f}_{\text{gated}} = \text{LN}_f\left( \mathbf{g} \odot \hat{\mathbf{s}} + (1 - \mathbf{g}) \odot \hat{\mathbf{v}} + \hat{\mathbf{s}} + \hat{\mathbf{v}} \right)
\end{equation}
where $\odot$ denotes element-wise multiplication and $\text{LN}_s$, $\text{LN}_v$, and $\text{LN}_f$ are LayerNorm~\cite{ba2016layernorm} layers (dropout is applied after $\text{LN}_f$). The residual term $\hat{\mathbf{s}} + \hat{\mathbf{v}}$ gives an effective per-dimension weighting of $(1 + \mathbf{g}) \odot \hat{\mathbf{s}} + (2 - \mathbf{g}) \odot \hat{\mathbf{v}}$, so the gate modulates each modality's \emph{relative} contribution over a baseline that always retains both, rather than suppressing either outright. The use of three LayerNorm layers (rather than BatchNorm) is motivated by the variable batch composition in MIL training and has implications for TTA, as discussed in Section~\ref{sec:tta}.

\subsection{MIL Training with Ranking Loss}

We adopt the MIL ranking loss formulation from Sultani et al.~\cite{sultani2018ucfcrime} with the refinements introduced by RTFM~\cite{tian2021rtfm}. Each training batch consists of paired normal and anomalous video bags. For each bag, snippet-level anomaly scores are computed by the classifier head, and the top-$k$ scoring snippets are selected. The ranking loss encourages the mean of the top-$k$ scores in the anomalous bag to exceed that of the normal bag by a margin:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{rank}} = \max\left(0,\; 1 - \frac{1}{k}\sum_{i \in \mathcal{A}_k} a_i + \frac{1}{k}\sum_{j \in \mathcal{N}_k} a_j\right)
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{A}_k$ and $\mathcal{N}_k$ are the top-$k$ snippet indices in the anomalous and normal bags, respectively, and $a$ denotes the predicted anomaly score. The total loss includes sparsity and temporal smoothness regularization terms:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{rank}} + \frac{\lambda_1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T} \left\| \mathbf{a}_t \right\|_2 + \lambda_2 \sum_i (a_i - a_{i+1})^2
    \label{eq:loss}
\end{equation}
Here $\mathbf{a}_t$ is the vector of abnormal-bag anomaly scores at snippet position $t$ across the batch, so the sparsity term---following the feature-magnitude formulation of RTFM~\cite{tian2021rtfm}---is the mean over snippet positions of the per-position $\ell_2$ norm, computed over the abnormal bag. The sparsity term encourages most snippets to receive low anomaly scores, reflecting the prior that anomalous events occupy a small fraction of each video's duration. The smoothness term penalizes rapid fluctuations in adjacent snippet scores, encouraging temporally coherent predictions. We set the sparsity weight $\lambda_1 = 8 \times 10^{-3}$; the smoothness weight $\lambda_2$ is dataset-dependent, as detailed in Section~\ref{sec:impl}.

During training, each batch consists of paired normal and anomalous video bags, with the training loader shuffling pairs to prevent class-ordering bias. The validation loader also uses shuffled batches to avoid single-class batch bias, which is important because the MIL ranking loss requires both normal and anomalous samples within each batch to compute the ranking gradient.

\subsection{Test-Time Discriminative-Reliability Reweighting}
\label{sec:disc}

At deployment the visual-language stream may degrade under input corruption while the skeleton stream remains comparatively robust. We adapt the gated fusion at test time, without labels or tuned hyperparameters, by reweighting the two streams according to a label-free estimate of visual-stream reliability. Let $p_{\mathrm{clip}}$ denote the LayerNorm-projected visual-language features. We estimate visual reliability from the dispersion of the visual stream's solo anomaly scores,
\begin{equation}
w_{\mathrm{vl}} = \min\!\Big(1,\ \sigma\big(s_{\mathrm{clip}}^{\mathrm{test}}\big) \big/ \sigma\big(s_{\mathrm{clip}}^{\mathrm{clean}}\big)\Big),
\end{equation}
where $s_{\mathrm{clip}}$ are the snippet scores produced by the fusion head with the skeleton stream zeroed and $\sigma(\cdot)$ is the standard deviation pooled over a corruption condition; $w_{\mathrm{vl}}\!\to\!0$ when corruption destroys the visual stream's ability to separate snippets and $w_{\mathrm{vl}}\!\to\!1$ when it is unaffected. A skeleton-reliability gate $s = \min\!\big(1, \max\big(0,\, (2 z_0 - z_{\mathrm{skel}})/z_0\big)\big)$ guards against this, where $z_{\mathrm{skel}}$ is the skeleton features' mean per-channel distribution shift (corruption condition vs.\ clean training) and $z_0$ is the analogous clean-train-to-clean-test shift used as a reference: $s\!\to\!1$ when the skeleton is no more shifted than the clean source data and $s\!\to\!0$ once its shift reaches $2 z_0$, so the fusion does not lean on a skeleton that is itself corrupted. The factor of two is a fixed anchor, not tuned to performance; under our corruptions $s$ saturates to $\{0,1\}$ (conditions that recompute the skeleton on degraded frames give $z_{\mathrm{skel}}\!\gg\!z_0$, those that reuse the clean skeleton cache give $z_{\mathrm{skel}}\!\approx\!z_0$), so its exact value does not affect the reported results. We scale the visual contribution by $w = 1-(1-w_{\mathrm{vl}})\,s$, applied to $p_{\mathrm{clip}}$ after its LayerNorm in both the gated and residual terms, so the fusion routes toward the corruption-robust skeleton exactly when the visual stream collapses and the skeleton remains clean. All statistics are pooled over the whole condition (transductive) and use no labels; the clean reference may be drawn from training or clean-test features.

\section{Experiments}

\subsection{Datasets}

\textbf{UCF-Crime}~\cite{sultani2018ucfcrime} contains 1,900 untrimmed surveillance videos spanning 13 anomaly categories (Abuse, Arrest, Arson, Assault, Burglary, Explosion, Fighting, RoadAccidents, Robbery, Shooting, Shoplifting, Stealing, Vandalism) plus a normal class. The training set contains 800 normal and 810 anomalous videos with only video-level binary labels. The test set contains 150 normal and 140 anomalous videos with frame-level temporal annotations, of which 254 meet the 64-frame minimum and are evaluated. Videos are sourced from real surveillance cameras and average approximately 4 minutes in length. We evaluate using frame-level Area Under the ROC Curve (AUC), following the standard protocol established by Sultani et al.~\cite{sultani2018ucfcrime}.

\textbf{XD-Violence}~\cite{wu2020xdviolence} is a larger multi-modal dataset containing 4,754 untrimmed videos across six violence categories: Fighting (B1), Shooting (B2), Riot (B4), Abuse (B5), Car Accident (B6), and Explosion (G). Unlike UCF-Crime's fixed-camera surveillance footage, XD-Violence draws from heterogeneous sources including movies, news broadcasts, sports events, and online media, resulting in greater visual diversity. The training set contains 3,954 videos and the test set contains 800 videos with frame-level annotations. We report Average Precision (AP) as the primary metric, following the benchmark protocol~\cite{wu2020xdviolence}.

\textbf{Data splits.} For both datasets, we hold out 15\% of the training set as a stratified validation split, maintaining the normal/anomalous ratio across splits. Because the training videos carry only video-level labels, the validation split is used for early stopping and per-run checkpoint selection via the validation MIL loss; frame-level model selection is not possible on this split. Following the standard UCF-Crime and XD-Violence evaluation protocol, the cross-backbone and ablation comparisons report frame-level test-set metrics. The same splits are used across all experiments and all backbone variants.

\subsection{Implementation Details}
\label{sec:impl}

All experiments are conducted on a single NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPU (24~GB). Skeleton features are extracted using RTMPose-m with YOLOX-m detection at approximately 21.6 FPS, followed by CTR-GCN encoding with NTU-120 pre-trained weights across all four streams (joint, bone, joint motion, bone motion). Visual features are extracted at 1~FPS with the respective frozen backbone.

The fusion head and anomaly classifier are trained using AdamW (weight decay $10^{-2}$) with a learning rate of $1 \times 10^{-4}$ for both datasets. The learning rate follows a linear warmup over the first 5 epochs, then cosine-annealing to zero. We use a batch size of 16 normal/anomalous pairs (32 videos per batch) and train for a maximum of 50 epochs with early stopping (patience of 10 epochs on validation loss). Each video is represented as a fixed-length bag of $T=32$ snippets (uniform segment sampling when longer, sampling with replacement when shorter). The top-$k$ selection in MIL ranking uses $k=3$ for both datasets. We use a fixed hyperparameter configuration across both datasets (learning rate $1 \times 10^{-4}$, $k=3$, margin $1.0$). The temporal smoothness weight $\lambda_2$ in Eq.~(\ref{eq:loss}) is the one dataset-dependent setting: we use $\lambda_2 = 8 \times 10^{-4}$ on UCF-Crime but $\lambda_2 = 0$ on XD-Violence. We found that penalizing adjacent-snippet score differences over-regularizes and destabilizes the AP metric on XD-Violence's heterogeneous, rapidly-cut sources (movies, news, sports), while providing a small benefit on UCF-Crime's continuous fixed-camera footage.\footnote{The reported UCF-Crime numbers were produced with an earlier implementation of the smoothness term; the released code applies the penalty strictly along the temporal (adjacent-snippet) axis. We verified that this change shifts the headline UCF-Crime AUC by at most 0.13 percentage points---within the 0.1--0.4\% seed variance reported below---so the UCF-Crime results are reported as run.}

For all fusion variants we report mean and standard deviation over three random seeds (42, 123, 2024) to quantify training stability. Snippet-level anomaly scores are expanded to frame-level predictions by assigning each frame the score of its enclosing 64-frame snippet. On UCF-Crime, the final snippet's score is repeated to cover any trailing frames of the full-length frame grid; on XD-Violence, evaluation is restricted to the snippet-aligned portion (a whole number of 64-frame windows), so any trailing partial window is dropped.

\subsection{Ablation Study}

Tables~\ref{tab:ucf-ablation} and~\ref{tab:xd-ablation} present the ablation results on UCF-Crime and XD-Violence, respectively, comparing six fusion variants across all four visual backbones. \textbf{Skeleton features provide complementary information.} The skeleton-only baseline achieves 68.8\% AUC on UCF-Crime and 40.8\% AP on XD-Violence---substantially below the visual-only baselines. However, this lower standalone performance does not diminish the skeleton modality's contribution to fusion. Gated fusion meets or exceeds the visual-only baseline in all eight backbone$\times$dataset configurations (mean $+0.6$ points; strictly positive in six of eight, the other two tied at the displayed precision, so a tie-dropping sign test over these eight---correlated, since they share one frozen skeleton stream---configurations gives $p\approx0.03$) and never degrades a strong visual backbone. The per-configuration gains are modest and individually within seed variance, but their consistency indicates that the skeleton stream supplies a small, reliable signal not captured by visual appearance alone---most pronounced on XD-Violence (up to $+2.1$ AP with SigLIP2 SO400M).

\textbf{Gated fusion outperforms late fusion consistently.} The gated fusion mechanism outperforms simple late fusion (equal-weight score averaging) on every backbone on \emph{both} datasets: the UCF-Crime margins are $+2.5$/$+1.8$/$+2.2$/$+2.6$ and the XD-Violence margins are $+12.6$/$+10.9$/$+13.0$/$+11.1$ points for CLIP/SigLIP2 Base/SO400M/Giant. This gap is particularly notable on XD-Violence, where late fusion with CLIP achieves only 63.9\% AP compared to 76.5\% for gated fusion---a difference of 12.6 percentage points. We attribute this to the learned gating mechanism's ability to weight modalities differently for each input, reducing the weaker modality's relative contribution when its signal is noisy.

\textbf{Late fusion can degrade performance.} A notable finding is that late fusion sometimes performs worse than the visual-only baseline. On UCF-Crime, CLIP late fusion (78.9\%) falls below CLIP visual-only (81.2\%), a drop of 2.3 percentage points. This suggests that the fixed equal weighting lets the weaker skeleton signal drag down every fused score, with no input-dependent mechanism to reduce its weight relative to the stronger visual stream. The gated mechanism resolves this by learning to modulate each modality's contribution.

Figure~\ref{fig:temporal-scores} illustrates the temporal behavior of our gated fusion model on the Fighting047 test video: within the annotated event window the fused score peaks above the visual-only stream, and in the subsequent normal segment it drops below that stream to near zero, a downward correction that equal-weight late fusion cannot make.

A key finding of our study is that \emph{backbone choice interacts with dataset characteristics} in ways that are not predicted by general-purpose benchmark rankings. Figure~\ref{fig:backbone-comparison} visualizes these interactions across all variants.

\textbf{UCF-Crime favors larger backbones.} On UCF-Crime, SigLIP2 Giant consistently achieves the highest scores across nearly all fusion variants: 82.4\% visual-only, 82.5\% gated fusion, and 82.5\% with mean-only pooling. The ranking follows model capacity---Giant $>$ SO400M $\approx$ CLIP $>$ SigLIP2 Base---suggesting that the 13-category surveillance setting benefits from richer visual representations.

\textbf{XD-Violence favors SO400M under fusion.} On XD-Violence the four backbones' visual-only AP is statistically indistinguishable across seeds (CLIP $74.6$, SigLIP2 Base $74.5$, SO400M $76.6$, Giant $76.8$; std $1.0$--$2.2$), so visual-only capacity does not cleanly rank them. Under gated fusion, however, SO400M emerges as the clear and stable leader ($78.7$\% AP), ahead of Giant ($76.8$\%). This may reflect SO400M's shape-optimized architecture providing a better capacity fit for XD-Violence's heterogeneous content (movies, sports, and online media) than for the fixed-camera surveillance of UCF-Crime.

\textbf{The skeleton's benefit is backbone-dependent.} Because the visual-only XD-Violence scores fall within seed variance, they do not reliably rank the backbones; gated fusion is more stable (std $0.9$\% AP for three of the four) and resolves a clear leader (SO400M, $78.7$\%) that the visual-only numbers do not single out. The gain that fusion adds over the visual-only baseline also varies with the backbone---largest for CLIP and SO400M ($+1.9$ and $+2.1$ AP) and negligible for SigLIP2 Base and Giant---so the skeleton stream's complementary value depends on the visual representation it is fused with rather than on raw visual capacity alone, a dependence that single-backbone evaluations would miss.

\textbf{Seed stability.} On UCF-Crime, Gated Fusion exhibits low variance across seeds for all backbones (standard deviation 0.1--0.4\% AUC), reflecting stable training dynamics on this dataset. On XD-Violence, three of the four backbones are comparably stable---CLIP, SigLIP2 Base, and SO400M all sit near 0.9\% AP standard deviation---while SigLIP2 Giant is the lone outlier at 2.8\%. This stability is tied to the dataset-dependent smoothness setting (Section~\ref{sec:impl}): disabling the temporal smoothness term on XD-Violence not only improves AP but also markedly tightens seed variance, indicating that the elevated variance seen in smoothness-regularized configurations is in part a regularization artifact rather than an intrinsic property of the dataset. The residual sensitivity of SigLIP2 Giant likely reflects (1)~AP's inherent sensitivity to score calibration relative to AUC, and (2)~XD-Violence's category imbalance and heterogeneous sources creating a more complex optimization landscape for the highest-capacity backbone.

We evaluate test-time adaptation on UCF-Crime-C, a corruption benchmark constructed by applying four corruption types (Gaussian noise, motion blur, JPEG compression, and brightness shift) at five severity levels to the UCF-Crime test set, yielding 20 corruption conditions in total. These corruptions simulate common real-world distribution shifts encountered in surveillance deployment: sensor noise from low-light cameras, motion blur from camera shake or moving objects, compression artifacts from bandwidth-limited transmission, and illumination changes from varying lighting conditions.

TENT~\cite{wang2021tent} and SAR~\cite{niu2023sar} adapt only the LayerNorm affine parameters ($\gamma,\beta$) of the gated fusion head---three LN layers totaling 1{,}536 parameters---under a deterministic, continual-online protocol that adapts across each per-condition corruption stream (TENT: $\text{lr}=10^{-3}$; SAR: $\text{lr}=10^{-3}$, $\rho=0.05$). All TTA results are means over three training seeds $\{42,123,2024\}$. Table~\ref{tab:tta} and Figure~\ref{fig:tta-comparison} present the results.

\textbf{Entropy minimization gives no benefit.} Under the deterministic protocol, TENT and SAR change mean AUC by less than $0.1$ percentage points on every backbone---effectively zero. This is not a tuning artifact: with the gate and MIL head frozen, only the LayerNorm affine parameters adapt, and the resulting updates are far too small to reorder the pooled snippet scores on which the rank-based AUC depends. The corruption-induced shift lives upstream in the frozen backbone features, which affine adaptation of the fusion head cannot reach.

\textbf{Feature-statistic restoration is rank-disruptive.} Aligning the corrupted test features' first- and second-order statistics to the clean-training statistics (NORM/CORAL-style) does reach the feature space, but degrades AUC: per-channel whitening reorders the discriminative directions for the SigLIP2 backbones, and the sign of the effect flips across backbones, so no single operating point selected without test labels is net-positive across the four backbones. The corruption shift is not correctable by a label-free affine or covariance map on the features.

\textbf{Discriminative-reliability reweighting recovers robustness.} We instead exploit the dual-modal structure (Section~\ref{sec:disc}). The gated fusion already mixes a frozen skeleton stream---robust to pixel-domain corruption---with the visual-language stream; under corruption we down-weight the visual-language contribution by a per-condition scalar $w$ when that stream is unreliable, leaning on the skeleton. Reliability is estimated label-free from the dispersion of the visual stream's solo anomaly scores, which collapses precisely when corruption destroys its ability to separate snippets, and is gated so the fusion does not lean on the skeleton when it too is corrupted. The estimate uses no labels and no tuned hyperparameter, and the clean reference may be drawn from training or clean-test features with equivalent results.

This improves mean AUC on all four backbones across the three seeds (Table~\ref{tab:tta}; overall mean $+1.21$ points), with every per-backbone gain positive on every seed. The improvement is concentrated on Gaussian noise ($+4.8$ points on average across backbones, up to $+13.2$ at the highest severity on SigLIP2-SO400M; Table~\ref{tab:tta_breakdown}), the corruption that most collapses the visual stream while leaving the skeleton untouched: for Gaussian and brightness the skeleton features are the clean cache, reused unchanged, so the gain is recovery by routing to an unaffected modality rather than intrinsic robustness of the fused features. It is near-zero by construction where the visual stream remains reliable (brightness) or the skeleton is also corrupted (motion blur, JPEG), as the gate then leaves the fusion unchanged. Because the effect is a routing toward the skeleton stream, it is bounded by the skeleton-fusion ceiling and can regress when the corrupted visual stream is still the stronger detector: on one seed SigLIP2-Base loses up to $3.2$ points at high-severity Gaussian, where its visual scores compress without losing rank and the spread proxy over-routes (this backbone is nonetheless positive on all three seeds on average). The reliability estimate is transductive---pooled over the whole corruption condition---so it assumes batched access to the corrupted stream rather than streaming operation, as is standard in the corruption-robustness setting.

\section{Discussion}

Our results reveal several patterns that merit discussion beyond the per-section analysis.

\textbf{The complementarity argument.} Despite achieving only 68.8\% AUC in isolation on UCF-Crime, the skeleton modality consistently improves gated fusion performance over visual-only baselines. This gap between standalone and fusion performance suggests that skeleton features provide \emph{orthogonal} information---motion dynamics not captured by appearance-based vision-language models---rather than redundant signals. The gated mechanism's large advantage over fixed equal-weight late fusion on XD-Violence (raising CLIP AP from 63.9\% to 76.5\%, a 12.6-point gap; Table~\ref{tab:xd-ablation}) indicates that the network extracts usable signal from the skeleton stream through input-dependent weighting, which a redundant or pure-noise modality could not provide.

\textbf{Dataset-specific backbone preferences.} The divergence between UCF-Crime and XD-Violence in optimal backbone selection (Giant for UCF-Crime, SO400M for XD-Violence under gated fusion) has practical implications for system deployment. UCF-Crime consists of fixed-camera surveillance footage where the larger Giant backbone's richer representations may better disambiguate fine-grained anomaly categories. XD-Violence draws from heterogeneous sources including movies, sports broadcasts, and news footage, where SO400M's shape-optimized architecture may provide a better capacity fit. This finding cautions against selecting a visual backbone based on general-purpose benchmarks alone and suggests that dataset-specific evaluation is essential.

\textbf{The late fusion failure mode.} The consistent underperformance of late fusion relative to both gated fusion and, in some cases, visual-only baselines warrants attention. Late fusion combines the two modalities with a fixed equal weight, so when the skeleton signal quality is low (as reflected by its 68.8\% standalone AUC on UCF-Crime and 40.8\% AP on XD-Violence) it still contributes half of every score, degrading inputs where the visual stream alone is more reliable. The gated mechanism instead learns an input-dependent, per-dimension reweighting over a residual baseline, allowing it to reduce the skeleton stream's relative contribution for inputs where visual features are more informative.

\textbf{TTA and the LayerNorm barrier.} Our TTA results are an informative negative finding with implications beyond VAD. Entropy-minimization methods such as TENT and SAR were designed for BatchNorm networks, where adaptation recalibrates the running mean and variance statistics that summarize the source distribution~\cite{wang2021tent}. Our fusion head normalizes with LayerNorm, which keeps no such running statistics---normalization is computed per sample---so on each batch these methods can only nudge the LayerNorm affine parameters ($\gamma$, $\beta$; 1{,}536 scalars in our head). With the visual-language backbone, the gate, and the detection head all frozen, the corruption-induced shift lives upstream in the frozen features, and affine updates of this size are far too small to reorder a rank-based AUC---leaving the metric essentially unchanged ($<0.1$ points). Forcing the features back toward the clean statistics directly (NORM/CORAL-style) does reach the right space but is rank-disruptive, with an effect whose sign flips across backbones. The lesson is not that the corruptions are benign, but that adaptation confined to LayerNorm affine parameters cannot move a frozen, rank-based detector. What does work here is to change \emph{which} modality the frozen fusion trusts: our label-free discriminative-reliability reweighting routes the gated fusion toward the corruption-robust skeleton stream when the visual-language stream's discriminative signal collapses, recovering corrupted-AUC on all four backbones without any parameter updates. This suggests that robustness for frozen LayerNorm-based fusion heads is better pursued through reliability-aware routing---or, more broadly, prompt- or adapter-based mechanisms that alter the features themselves---than through affine test-time adaptation.

\textbf{Computational efficiency.} Our frozen-backbone design yields a lightweight trainable component: the gated fusion head contains 0.50M (CLIP) to 1.02M (SigLIP2 Giant) trainable parameters, scaling with the visual feature dimension $d_v$, as only the projection, gating, and MIL detection-head layers are learned. Training completes in under 5 minutes per configuration on a single RTX 4090. This efficiency enables the comprehensive multi-backbone evaluation presented here, which would be infeasible with end-to-end backbone fine-tuning.

\section{Limitations and Future Work}

Our study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the results.

\textbf{Performance gaps.} Our best UCF-Crime result (82.5\% AUC with SigLIP2 Giant) falls below the published performance of methods that use end-to-end fine-tuned backbones or temporal modeling specifically designed for anomaly detection. This gap reflects our architectural choice to freeze all backbone encoders, which prioritizes computational efficiency and modularity over peak performance. Unfreezing selected backbone layers or incorporating temporal transformers could improve absolute numbers but would increase training cost substantially on our single-GPU setup.

Table~\ref{tab:comparison} groups representative published results by operating regime rather than as a single ranking. We list the strongest published methods on each benchmark together with all frozen-feature, no-text peers, so the comparison is not restricted to favorable rows: the upper block uses heavier budgets (a text-alignment branch, auxiliary training-time modalities, or a multimodal LLM), while the lower block is our comparable regime---frozen features, no text branch, a single-GPU-class head. Within that comparable regime, our 78.7\% XD-Violence AP is on par with RTFM~\cite{tian2021rtfm} (77.81\%) and the I3D variant of MGFN~\cite{chen2023mgfn} (79.19\%), and above the founding Sultani et al.~\cite{sultani2018ucfcrime} baseline (75.41\%). On UCF-Crime our 82.5\% clears Sultani but trails the other frozen-feature methods (RTFM 84.30\%, Light-WVAD 84.7\%, UR-DMU~\cite{zhou2023urdmu} 86.97\%, MGFN 86.98\%, CLIP-TSA~\cite{joo2023cliptsa} 87.58\%), so our competitiveness within this regime is clearest on XD-Violence AP. We attribute the residual UCF gap to CLIP-TSA---the closest frozen-CLIP analogue---to the temporal self-attention and multi-crop aggregation it adds and our minimal head omits, but we do not isolate this experimentally and leave a controlled ablation to future work. The upper block also shows that a heavier budget is not automatically decisive: the recent training-free methods EventVAD~\cite{shao2025eventvad} and LAVAD~\cite{zanella2024lavad} run a 7B and 13B multimodal LLM (on an 80\,GB A800 and on dual RTX 3090s, respectively) yet reach 82.03\% and 80.28\% UCF---at or below our 82.5\%---and fall far below us on XD-Violence AP (64.04\% / 62.01\% vs 78.7\%); our frozen dual-modal head on a single 24\,GB GPU is thus a markedly cheaper operating point at comparable or better accuracy.

\textbf{Seed sensitivity on XD-Violence.} XD-Violence gated fusion shows elevated seed variance only for the highest-capacity backbone (SigLIP2 Giant, 2.8\% AP standard deviation); the other three backbones are stable (near 0.9\%), comparable to UCF-Crime (maximum 0.4\%). Future work should investigate ensemble strategies or variance-reduction techniques for more stable training.

\textbf{TTA scope.} Our test-time method targets the regime where one modality stays robust; its gains are concentrated on corruptions that collapse the visual-language stream (e.g., Gaussian noise) and are near-zero, by construction, where both streams degrade (motion blur, JPEG). It relies on transductive whole-condition statistics and on a discriminative-spread reliability proxy that can over-route on backbones whose visual stream compresses scores without losing rank (SigLIP2-Base at high-severity Gaussian on one seed). Streaming/causal variants, and a label-free signal that distinguishes score compression from rank loss, are left to future work.

\textbf{Dataset scope.} Our evaluation is limited to two datasets (UCF-Crime and XD-Violence). Generalization to other VAD benchmarks such as ShanghaiTech or CUHK Avenue, which feature different anomaly types (e.g., pedestrian trajectory anomalies rather than violent actions) and different video characteristics, remains to be verified. Additionally, our corruption conditions for TTA are synthetically generated; real-world distribution shifts involving camera aging, environmental changes, or cross-city deployment may exhibit different characteristics.

\textbf{Future directions.} Several extensions of this work are promising:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Exploring attention-based temporal fusion across snippets to capture longer-range temporal dependencies beyond the current 64-frame window.
    \item Investigating adapter-based TTA strategies (e.g., LoRA-style low-rank adaptation or visual prompt tuning) that can capture distributional signals without relying on normalization layer parameters.
    \item Extending the multi-backbone comparison to additional vision-language models as they become available, including models with native video understanding capabilities.
    \item Incorporating audio features for XD-Violence, which provides audio annotations that our current visual-skeleton pipeline does not exploit, potentially improving detection of categories like Shooting and Explosion where audio cues are highly discriminative.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion}

We have presented a dual-modal fusion framework for weakly supervised video anomaly detection that combines frozen skeleton dynamics from CTR-GCN with frozen vision-language features through a learned gated mechanism trained with MIL ranking loss. Our systematic study across four visual backbones---CLIP ViT-B/16 and three SigLIP2 variants---on UCF-Crime and XD-Violence yields three main findings.

First, skeleton features add a small but consistent complementary gain over visual-only baselines---meeting or exceeding them in all eight backbone$\times$dataset configurations (strictly positive in six), though modest and backbone-dependent in magnitude---despite the skeleton modality's substantially lower standalone performance (68.8\% AUC on UCF-Crime). Learned input-dependent gating also substantially outperforms fixed equal-weight late fusion on XD-Violence (by more than 10 AP points on every backbone), confirming the value of weighting modalities per input rather than with a static mix.

Second, backbone choice interacts with dataset characteristics in non-trivial ways. SigLIP2 Giant achieves the best UCF-Crime results (82.5\% AUC with gated fusion, 82.5\% with mean-only pooling), while SigLIP2 SO400M leads on XD-Violence under gated fusion (78.7\% AP); the visual-only XD-Violence backbones are within seed variance, so this preference emerges through fusion rather than from visual-only strength. These dataset-specific preferences underscore the importance of multi-backbone evaluation for VAD system design.

Third, our test-time adaptation study shows that entropy-minimization methods (TENT, SAR) cannot improve rank-based VAD metrics, and that a simple label-free \emph{discriminative-reliability reweighting}---routing the gated fusion toward the corruption-robust skeleton stream when the visual-language stream's discriminative signal collapses---recovers corrupted-AUC on all four backbones (mean $+1.2$ points, up to $+13.2$ points in the single most visually-degrading condition), concentrated where one modality remains reliable.

Our framework provides a modular, computationally efficient approach to multi-modal VAD that requires training only the lightweight fusion head (0.5--1.0M parameters depending on backbone) while leveraging frozen pre-trained encoders. The frozen-backbone design enables rapid evaluation of new vision-language models as they become available, without retraining the entire pipeline. The codebase and experiment configurations will be released to support reproducibility and facilitate future multi-backbone VAD studies.

Together, these findings demonstrate that the combination of skeleton dynamics and visual-language semantics, coupled with careful backbone selection and honest evaluation of adaptation techniques, advances our understanding of practical weakly supervised violence detection systems.
