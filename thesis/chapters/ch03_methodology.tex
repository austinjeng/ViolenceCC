\chapter{Methodology}
\label{ch:03}

This chapter presents the dual-modal fusion framework in full. Section~\ref{sec:meth-overview}
gives an end-to-end overview of the pipeline. Sections~\ref{sec:meth-skeleton}
and~\ref{sec:meth-ctrgcn} describe the skeleton stream---keypoint extraction and graph-convolutional
encoding---and Section~\ref{sec:meth-vlm} the visual-language stream. Section~\ref{sec:meth-fusion}
defines the two fusion mechanisms under study, Section~\ref{sec:meth-mil} the weakly supervised
training objective, and Section~\ref{sec:meth-tta} the test-time adaptation methods, including the
proposed discriminative-reliability reweighting (Section~\ref{sec:disc}). All equations in this
chapter are stated exactly as implemented in the released code.

\section{Pipeline Overview}
\label{sec:meth-overview}

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \resizebox{\textwidth}{!}{\input{figures/fig_architecture}}
  \caption{Overview of the dual-modal fusion pipeline. Two parallel \emph{frozen} streams extract complementary snippet features: RTMPose keypoints (top-2 persons) are encoded by a frozen CTR-GCN into 256-d skeleton features, while frames sampled at ${\sim}1$~FPS are embedded by a frozen vision-language backbone (CLIP ViT-B/16 or a SigLIP2 variant) and aggregated by concatenated mean+max pooling into a $2d$-dimensional (1024--3072-d) visual feature. The gated fusion module---together with the MIL head, the only trained component---projects each modality to a shared 256-d space (per-modality LayerNorm), blends them with a per-dimension learned gate, and retains both projected modalities through a residual connection followed by a third LayerNorm. The dashed annotation marks the test-time reliability reweighting that scales the visual stream by $w$ under corruption (Sec.~\ref{sec:disc}). The MIL head scores each snippet and is trained with the MIL ranking loss. Both streams share identical snippet boundaries, so skeleton and visual features are aligned by construction (Section~\ref{sec:meth-ctrgcn}).}
  \label{fig:architecture}
\end{figure}

Our framework processes surveillance videos through two parallel feature extraction
streams---skeleton-based and visual-language-based---whose outputs are combined via a learned
fusion mechanism and trained with MIL ranking loss. Figure~\ref{fig:architecture} illustrates the
complete pipeline. Both backbone encoders are \emph{frozen}: the CTR-GCN skeleton encoder retains
its NTU-pretrained weights, and the vision-language backbone retains its published pretraining
weights. Only the fusion head and the anomaly-scoring head are trainable.

The frozen-backbone design serves three purposes simultaneously. First, it respects the hardware
constraint of this thesis (a single RTX 4090), under which end-to-end fine-tuning of a
vision-language backbone at video scale is infeasible. Second, it isolates the experimental
variable: because the backbones never change, every performance difference between fusion
variants---and every difference across visual backbones---is attributable to the representation
and the small learned head, not to divergent fine-tuning trajectories. Third, it makes the
multi-backbone study of Chapter~\ref{ch:05} tractable: features are extracted once per video and
cached, after which each of the dozens of training configurations completes in minutes.

Concretely, each video is segmented into non-overlapping 64-frame snippets shared by both
streams. The skeleton stream converts each snippet into a 256-dimensional feature vector
(Sections~\ref{sec:meth-skeleton}--\ref{sec:meth-ctrgcn}); the visual stream converts the same
snippet into a $2d$-dimensional vector, where $d$ is the backbone's embedding width
(Section~\ref{sec:meth-vlm}). The fusion head maps the pair of snippet features to a single
anomaly score per snippet (Section~\ref{sec:meth-fusion}), and training uses only video-level
labels through the MIL ranking objective (Section~\ref{sec:meth-mil}).

\textbf{Notation.} Bold lowercase symbols denote vectors: $\mathbf{s} \in \mathbb{R}^{256}$ is a
skeleton snippet feature and $\mathbf{v} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_v}$ a visual snippet feature, with
$d_v = 2d$ for backbone embedding width $d$. Hatted symbols ($\hat{\mathbf{s}}$,
$\hat{\mathbf{v}}$) denote projected and normalized versions of the same quantities. Scalar $a$
denotes a snippet anomaly score, $T$ the fixed bag length used in training, and $k$ the top-$k$
selection size of the ranking loss. The three LayerNorm modules of the fusion head are named
$\text{LN}_s$, $\text{LN}_v$, and $\text{LN}_f$ (skeleton projection, visual projection, and
fused output respectively); these names are used consistently through the test-time adaptation
analysis of Section~\ref{sec:meth-tta} and Chapter~\ref{ch:06}.

\section{Skeleton Extraction with RTMPose}
\label{sec:meth-skeleton}

We extract 2D human pose keypoints from video frames using RTMPose-m~\cite{jiang2023rtmpose}, a
real-time multi-person pose estimation model based on the top-down paradigm. The extraction
pipeline proceeds in two stages: (1)~a YOLOX-m object detector localizes person bounding boxes in
each frame, and (2)~RTMPose-m estimates 17 COCO-format keypoints (nose, eyes, ears, shoulders,
elbows, wrists, hips, knees, ankles) for each detected person. The top-down design---detect
persons first, then estimate pose inside each box---is preferable to bottom-up alternatives for
surveillance footage, where person scale varies widely across the frame and per-box pose
estimation normalizes that variation away. RTMPose-m occupies a deliberate accuracy--throughput
operating point: accurate enough for reliable keypoints on medium-resolution surveillance frames,
fast enough that extracting poses for hundreds of hours of video on one GPU remains practical.

We retain the top-2 persons per frame ranked by mean keypoint confidence to maintain a fixed
spatial dimension across all inputs. Two persons is a natural minimum for violence-oriented
detection---fights, assaults, and robberies are typically interactions between at least two
people---while keeping the tensor layout fixed and small. When fewer than 2 persons are detected,
the remaining slots are zero-padded. The raw pixel coordinates are normalized to the $[-1, 1]$
range about the image center via the PreNormalize2D transformation, matching the input convention
of the NTU-pretrained CTR-GCN encoder; without this step, the frozen encoder would receive
coordinates on a scale it never saw during pretraining.

The extracted skeletons are organized as tensors of shape $(T_f, M, V, C)$ where $T_f$ is the
number of frames, $M=2$ is the maximum person count, $V=17$ is the number of joints, and $C=3$
includes the $(x, y)$ coordinates and a keypoint confidence score. The confidence channel is
retained deliberately: it lets the downstream encoder discount uncertain keypoints (occlusions,
motion blur, small scale) rather than treating all coordinates as equally reliable. The
extraction runs at approximately 21.6 FPS on single-person scenes (about 15 FPS with multiple
persons) on a single RTX 4090 using ONNX Runtime with CUDA acceleration; pose extraction
dominates the offline preprocessing cost of the entire pipeline.

\section{Skeleton Feature Encoding with CTR-GCN}
\label{sec:meth-ctrgcn}

Skeleton sequences are encoded using CTR-GCN~\cite{chen2021ctrgcn}, a graph convolutional network
that refines the graph topology in a channel-wise manner. Fixed-topology GCNs such as
ST-GCN~\cite{yan2018stgcn} assume a static skeleton graph derived from the anatomical bone
structure, which constrains every feature channel to aggregate over the same joint neighborhoods.
CTR-GCN instead learns channel-specific topology refinement matrices that adapt the adjacency
structure for different feature channels, enabling the network to capture both local joint
interactions (for example, wrist--elbow dynamics during a punch) and global body configurations
(overall posture during a fall) in different channels of the same layer.

We employ a CTR-GCN model pre-trained on NTU RGB+D 120~\cite{yan2022pyskl} for general action
recognition, using it as a frozen feature extractor. The model processes four complementary input
streams: joint positions (j), bone vectors (b), joint motion (jm), and bone motion (bm). Joint
positions encode absolute spatial coordinates, bone vectors capture relative limb orientations,
and the motion streams encode first-order temporal differences of each. Each stream produces a
256-dimensional feature vector per temporal window, and the four are combined by weighted
averaging with weights 1.0 (joint), 1.0 (bone), 0.5 (joint motion), and 0.5 (bone motion)---the
static streams carry the primary signal, while the motion streams contribute a smaller refinement,
following the relative stream strengths observed in skeleton action recognition practice.

Following the PYSKL~\cite{yan2022pyskl} convention, we segment each video into non-overlapping 64-frame snippets and pool the backbone output over the person ($M$), temporal ($T_f$), and spatial ($V$) dimensions via global average pooling to produce a compact 256-d snippet representation. The four-stream features are combined by weighted averaging (joint and bone weighted 1.0, motion streams 0.5) to form the final 256-d skeleton representation. Videos shorter than 64 frames (172 videos in UCF-Crime, one in XD-Violence) are excluded from training and evaluation. Because UCF-Crime is distributed as frames pre-sampled at roughly 3~FPS (every tenth frame of its ${\sim}30$~FPS source video) while XD-Violence is decoded at its native ${\sim}24$~FPS, a 64-frame snippet spans about 20~s on UCF-Crime versus ${\sim}2.7$~s on XD-Violence; both streams use identical snippet boundaries, so the skeleton and visual features stay aligned by construction. (On UCF-Crime a single snippet thus pools skeleton motion over ${\sim}20$~s---far longer than the few-second action clips on which CTR-GCN was pre-trained---a plausible factor in the weaker UCF skeleton-only result reported below.)

Two consequences of this design deserve emphasis. First, because both streams use identical
snippet boundaries, no temporal registration step is needed anywhere in the pipeline: the fusion
head always receives a skeleton feature and a visual feature describing exactly the same frames.
Second, the frozen NTU-pretrained encoder was trained on short, single-action indoor clips; the
20-second effective snippet duration on UCF-Crime stretches this encoder well outside its
pretraining regime, an honest caveat for interpreting the skeleton-only results of
Chapter~\ref{ch:05}.

\section{Visual-Language Feature Extraction}
\label{sec:meth-vlm}

We extract visual features from video frames using frozen vision-language backbones. We evaluate
four models spanning two architectural families:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{CLIP ViT-B/16}~\cite{radford2021clip}: The original contrastive language-image
    model with a Vision Transformer (ViT-B/16) image encoder~\cite{dosovitskiy2021vit}, producing
    512-d frame-level features.
    \item \textbf{SigLIP2 ViT-B/16}~\cite{tschannen2025siglip2}: A sigmoid-loss variant trained on
    multilingual data with improved dense prediction capabilities, producing 768-d features.
    \item \textbf{SigLIP2 SO400M}: A shape-optimized ${\sim}400$M-parameter SigLIP2 encoder
    (SoViT-400M), producing 1152-d features.
    \item \textbf{SigLIP2 Giant}: The largest variant in the SigLIP2 family with an optimized
    giant architecture, producing 1536-d features.
\end{itemize}
All four models share the Vision Transformer~\cite{dosovitskiy2021vit} architecture but differ in training objective, data scale, and embedding dimensionality. CLIP uses contrastive loss on 400M image-text pairs, while SigLIP2 employs a sigmoid-based pairwise loss and is trained on substantially larger multilingual corpora. The three SigLIP2 backbones use the open-clip checkpoints \texttt{ViT-B-16-SigLIP2-256}, \texttt{ViT-SO400M-16-SigLIP2-256}, and \texttt{ViT-gopt-16-SigLIP2-256} (all pretrained on WebLI), while CLIP ViT-B/16 uses the original OpenAI weights.

These four backbones were selected to span two axes at once: training objective (CLIP's softmax
contrastive loss versus SigLIP2's sigmoid pairwise loss with auxiliary objectives) and capacity
(embedding widths from 512-d to 1536-d, encoder parameters across roughly an order of magnitude).
Holding the entire remainder of the pipeline fixed while varying only this component is what
allows Chapter~\ref{ch:05} to attribute performance differences to the backbone itself.

For each 64-frame snippet, we extract frame-level features at approximately 1~FPS, yielding
roughly 3 frames per snippet on XD-Violence and about 20 on UCF-Crime, reflecting the
dataset-dependent snippet duration described in Section~\ref{sec:meth-ctrgcn}. The frame-level
features are aggregated via concatenated mean and max pooling, producing a $2d$-dimensional
snippet representation (e.g., 1024-d for CLIP's 512-d features, 3072-d for SigLIP2 Giant's 1536-d
features). Mean pooling captures the average semantic content of the snippet, while max pooling
preserves the most salient per-dimension activations, which may correspond to the most anomalous
frame within the temporal window. The concatenation lets the fusion head weigh these two
summaries independently rather than forcing a single pooling prior; the contribution of the max
branch is ablated in Chapter~\ref{ch:05} (the ``mean-only'' pooling variant).

\section{Fusion Mechanisms}
\label{sec:meth-fusion}

We investigate two fusion strategies for combining skeleton features $\mathbf{s} \in
\mathbb{R}^{256}$ with visual features $\mathbf{v} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_v}$, where $d_v = 2d$ is the
dimension of the concatenated mean--max visual representation (with $d$ the backbone embedding
dimension). The two strategies sit at opposite ends of a design axis: late fusion commits to a
fixed, input-independent combination rule, while gated fusion learns an input-dependent,
per-dimension weighting.

\textbf{Late Fusion.} The simplest approach scores each modality independently and averages the
two snippet-level anomaly scores. Each modality is passed through its own classifier
head---$h_s(\cdot)$ for the skeleton stream and $h_v(\cdot)$ for the visual stream---and the
resulting per-snippet scores are combined with a fixed equal weight:
\begin{equation}
    a_{\text{late}} = \tfrac{1}{2}\, h_s(\mathbf{s}) + \tfrac{1}{2}\, h_v(\mathbf{v})
\end{equation}
This serves as a baseline that treats both modalities equally with a fixed weighting and no
input-dependent adaptation. Its appeal is simplicity---the two heads can even be trained
separately---but the fixed $\tfrac{1}{2}$ weight embeds a strong assumption: that both modalities
are equally reliable on every input. Chapter~\ref{ch:05} shows this assumption failing in
practice, with late fusion at times dragging performance below the visual-only baseline.

\textbf{Gated Fusion.} To enable the network to learn input-dependent modality weighting, we
employ a gated fusion mechanism. Each modality is first projected to a shared 256-d space and
normalized:
\begin{equation}
    \hat{\mathbf{s}} = \text{LN}_s(W_s \mathbf{s} + \mathbf{b}_s), \quad \hat{\mathbf{v}} = \text{LN}_v(W_v \mathbf{v} + \mathbf{b}_v)
\end{equation}
Projection to a shared space is necessary because the raw feature dimensions are highly
asymmetric ($256$ versus up to $3072$); the per-modality LayerNorms $\text{LN}_s$ and
$\text{LN}_v$ additionally equalize the scale of the two projected representations, so that
neither modality dominates the subsequent gate computation merely through feature magnitude.

A gating vector $\mathbf{g} \in [0, 1]^{256}$ is computed per dimension from the concatenated
projected features:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{g} = \sigma\left( W_g \left[ \hat{\mathbf{s}} \| \hat{\mathbf{v}} \right] + \mathbf{b}_g \right)
\end{equation}
The gate is per-dimension rather than scalar: different feature dimensions can encode different
aspects of the scene (motion patterns, object semantics, context), and a scalar gate would force
a single trust level across all of them. Because $\mathbf{g}$ is computed from both projected
modalities jointly, the gate can react to the \emph{relationship} between the two streams on each
input---for example, discounting the skeleton dimensions when no reliable pose was detected.

The fused representation applies the gated blend together with a residual connection that retains
both projected modalities, followed by dropout:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{f}_{\text{gated}} = \text{LN}_f\left( \mathbf{g} \odot \hat{\mathbf{s}} + (1 - \mathbf{g}) \odot \hat{\mathbf{v}} + \hat{\mathbf{s}} + \hat{\mathbf{v}} \right)
\end{equation}
where $\odot$ denotes element-wise multiplication and $\text{LN}_s$, $\text{LN}_v$, and
$\text{LN}_f$ are LayerNorm~\cite{ba2016layernorm} layers (dropout is applied after
$\text{LN}_f$). The residual term $\hat{\mathbf{s}} + \hat{\mathbf{v}}$ gives an effective
per-dimension weighting of $(1 + \mathbf{g}) \odot \hat{\mathbf{s}} + (2 - \mathbf{g}) \odot
\hat{\mathbf{v}}$, so the gate modulates each modality's \emph{relative} contribution over a
baseline that always retains both, rather than suppressing either outright. This bounded-gate
design is a safeguard: even a poorly calibrated gate cannot entirely delete a modality, so the
worst case degrades gracefully toward an (unequal-weight) additive fusion rather than toward a
single-modality model.

The fusion head thus contains exactly three named LayerNorm modules---$\text{LN}_s$ and
$\text{LN}_v$ on the two projected streams, and $\text{LN}_f$ on the fused output. The use of
LayerNorm rather than BatchNorm~\cite{ioffe2015batchnorm} is motivated by the variable batch
composition in MIL training: batches mix normal and anomalous bags whose score statistics differ
systematically, so batch-level statistics would be unstable and semantically contaminated,
whereas LayerNorm normalizes each snippet independently. This choice has structural consequences
for test-time adaptation---LayerNorm maintains no running statistics that could be recalibrated
under distribution shift---which are developed in Section~\ref{sec:meth-tta} and evaluated in
Chapter~\ref{ch:06}.

\section{MIL Training with Ranking Loss}
\label{sec:meth-mil}

We adopt the MIL ranking loss formulation from Sultani et al.~\cite{sultani2018ucfcrime} with the
refinements introduced by RTFM~\cite{tian2021rtfm}. Each training batch consists of paired normal
and anomalous video bags; each bag is a fixed-length sequence of $T=32$ snippet features (uniform
segment sampling when the video is longer, sampling with replacement when shorter). For each bag,
snippet-level anomaly scores are computed by the classifier head, and the top-$k$ scoring
snippets are selected. Selecting the top-$k$ (rather than only the single maximum) makes the
supervision signal less brittle to a single spuriously high-scoring snippet, while keeping the
weak-label semantics: an anomalous video need only contain \emph{some} anomalous snippets. The
ranking loss encourages the mean of the top-$k$ scores in the anomalous bag to exceed that of the
normal bag by a margin:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\text{rank}} = \max\left(0,\; 1 - \frac{1}{k}\sum_{i \in \mathcal{A}_k} a_i + \frac{1}{k}\sum_{j \in \mathcal{N}_k} a_j\right)
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{A}_k$ and $\mathcal{N}_k$ are the top-$k$ snippet indices in the anomalous and
normal bags, respectively, and $a$ denotes the predicted anomaly score. The total loss includes
sparsity and temporal smoothness regularization terms:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{rank}} + \frac{\lambda_1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T} \left\| \mathbf{a}_t \right\|_2 + \lambda_2 \sum_i (a_i - a_{i+1})^2
    \label{eq:loss}
\end{equation}
Here $\mathbf{a}_t$ is the vector of abnormal-bag anomaly scores at snippet position $t$ across
the batch, so the sparsity term---following the feature-magnitude formulation of
RTFM~\cite{tian2021rtfm}---is the mean over snippet positions of the per-position $\ell_2$ norm,
computed over the abnormal bag. The sparsity term encourages most snippets to receive low anomaly
scores, reflecting the prior that anomalous events occupy a small fraction of each video's
duration. The smoothness term penalizes rapid fluctuations in adjacent snippet scores,
encouraging temporally coherent predictions. We set the sparsity weight $\lambda_1 = 8 \times
10^{-3}$; the smoothness weight $\lambda_2$ is dataset-dependent, as detailed in
Chapter~\ref{ch:04}.

During training, each batch consists of paired normal and anomalous video bags, with the training
loader shuffling pairs to prevent class-ordering bias. The validation loader also uses shuffled
batches to avoid single-class batch bias, which is important because the MIL ranking loss
requires both normal and anomalous samples within each batch to compute the ranking gradient: a
sequentially ordered validation set would produce long runs of single-class batches whose
validation loss is degenerate, silently corrupting early stopping and checkpoint selection.
Because training labels exist only at video level, model selection uses the validation MIL loss;
frame-level information plays no role in any training or selection decision.

\section{Test-Time Adaptation Methods}
\label{sec:meth-tta}

At deployment, surveillance input degrades in ways the training data does not cover: sensor
noise, motion blur, compression artifacts, and lighting changes. This section defines the
test-time adaptation methods studied in this thesis; their empirical evaluation on the
UCF-Crime-C corruption benchmark appears in Chapter~\ref{ch:06}. Throughout, the deployed model
is the trained gated-fusion detector of Sections~\ref{sec:meth-fusion}--\ref{sec:meth-mil} with
\emph{all} learned parameters frozen; adaptation may use only unlabeled test data.

\subsection{Entropy Minimization on LayerNorm Parameters: TENT and SAR}
\label{sec:meth-tta-entropy}

TENT~\cite{wang2021tent} performs fully test-time adaptation by minimizing the entropy of the
model's predictions on each test batch, updating only normalization-layer parameters. In its
original BatchNorm setting, adaptation acts through two channels at once: the BatchNorm layers
re-estimate activation statistics on the shifted test data, and gradient descent on the entropy
objective updates the affine parameters ($\gamma$, $\beta$). SAR~\cite{niu2023sar} hardens this
recipe with reliability filtering---samples whose prediction entropy exceeds a margin are
excluded from the update---and a sharpness-aware gradient step~\cite{foret2021sam} that seeks
flat minima of the entropy surface.

Transferring these methods to our architecture requires three adaptations, implemented in
\texttt{src/tta/tent.py} and \texttt{src/tta/sar.py} (ported from the official SAR repository).
First, the adaptation surface changes: the parameter collection targets \texttt{nn.LayerNorm}
modules instead of BatchNorm. In the gated fusion head this yields exactly three LayerNorm
modules ($\text{LN}_s$, $\text{LN}_v$, $\text{LN}_f$; Section~\ref{sec:meth-fusion}), each
contributing a 256-d weight and a 256-d bias---six tensors totaling 1{,}536 adapted scalars. All
other parameters, including the vision-language backbone, the CTR-GCN encoder, the projections,
the gate, and the MIL head, remain frozen. Notably, LayerNorm removes one of BatchNorm's two
adaptation channels entirely: there are no running statistics to re-estimate, so entropy descent
on the affine parameters is the \emph{only} mechanism available. Second, the objective becomes
binary entropy, $-(s\log s + (1-s)\log(1-s))$ per snippet score $s$, since the MIL head outputs
sigmoid anomaly scores rather than a softmax class distribution; SAR's reliability margin is
rescaled accordingly to the binary-entropy range. Third, adaptation is made deterministic: the
model is placed in training mode only to enable gradient flow through the LayerNorms, while
every dropout module is explicitly kept in evaluation mode, so that adaptation results are
bit-reproducible across reruns.

Two adaptation protocols are considered. Under the \emph{episodic} (per-video) protocol, the
adapted parameters are reset to their source values after each video---the conservative protocol
that prevents error accumulation. On UCF-Crime, however, this protocol is structurally inert:
98.4\% of test videos contain at most $T=32$ snippets and are therefore scored in a single
forward chunk, so the parameter update triggered by a video's own batch can never affect that
video's already-computed scores, and the subsequent reset discards it. Under the
\emph{continual-online} protocol---the setting TENT and SAR were designed for---parameters are
reset once per corruption stream and adaptation accumulates across the videos of that stream, so
that updates from earlier videos affect the scoring of later ones. Chapter~\ref{ch:06} reports
results under the continual protocol, with the episodic inertness finding disclosed as part of
the protocol analysis.

\subsection{Feature-Statistic Restoration: NORM and CORAL}
\label{sec:meth-tta-restore}

If parameter adaptation cannot reach the corruption-induced shift, a second family of methods
acts on the features themselves. Statistic-restoration approaches estimate summary statistics of
the clean training features---per-channel means and standard deviations in the NORM
tradition~\cite{schneider2020norm}, or the full feature covariance in the CORAL
tradition~\cite{sun2016coral}---and, at test time, transform the corrupted features so that
their statistics match the clean reference. In the per-channel form, each corrupted feature
channel is standardized by the corrupted condition's statistics and re-scaled to the clean
statistics; in the covariance form, features are whitened with the corrupted covariance and
re-colored with the clean covariance. Applied to the pooled visual features of our pipeline,
both forms are label-free and, like the reweighting method below, transductive (statistics
pooled per corruption condition).

The appeal of this family is that---unlike LayerNorm-affine updates---it operates directly in
the space where the corruption shift actually lives. Its structural risk, however, is that a
transformation chosen to match feature \emph{moments} is under no obligation to preserve the
\emph{ordering} of the detector's output scores: whitening can rotate or rescale exactly the
directions the frozen detection head relies on to separate anomalous from normal snippets.
Chapter~\ref{ch:06} shows that this risk is realized in practice---restoration proves
rank-disruptive, with an effect whose sign flips across backbones---which motivates the method
of the next subsection.

\subsection{Test-Time Discriminative-Reliability Reweighting}
\label{sec:disc}

The entropy-minimization route adapts parameters; the feature-statistic restoration route
(Section~\ref{sec:meth-tta-restore}) moves features. The method proposed in this thesis does
neither: it changes \emph{which modality
the frozen fusion trusts}, exploiting the dual-modal structure that single-stream TTA methods do
not have. It updates zero parameters, uses no labels, and has no tuned hyperparameters.

At deployment the visual-language stream may degrade under input corruption while the skeleton stream remains comparatively robust. We adapt the gated fusion at test time, without labels or tuned hyperparameters, by reweighting the two streams according to a label-free estimate of visual-stream reliability. Let $p_{\mathrm{clip}}$ denote the LayerNorm-projected visual-language features. We estimate visual reliability from the dispersion of the visual stream's solo anomaly scores,
\begin{equation}
w_{\mathrm{vl}} = \min\!\Big(1,\ \sigma\big(s_{\mathrm{clip}}^{\mathrm{test}}\big) \big/ \sigma\big(s_{\mathrm{clip}}^{\mathrm{clean}}\big)\Big),
\end{equation}
where $s_{\mathrm{clip}}$ are the snippet scores produced by the fusion head with the skeleton stream zeroed and $\sigma(\cdot)$ is the standard deviation pooled over a corruption condition; $w_{\mathrm{vl}}\!\to\!0$ when corruption destroys the visual stream's ability to separate snippets and $w_{\mathrm{vl}}\!\to\!1$ when it is unaffected. A skeleton-reliability gate $s = \min\!\big(1, \max\big(0,\, (2 z_0 - z_{\mathrm{skel}})/z_0\big)\big)$ guards against this, where $z_{\mathrm{skel}}$ is the skeleton features' mean per-channel distribution shift (corruption condition vs.\ clean training) and $z_0$ is the analogous clean-train-to-clean-test shift used as a reference: $s\!\to\!1$ when the skeleton is no more shifted than the clean source data and $s\!\to\!0$ once its shift reaches $2 z_0$, so the fusion does not lean on a skeleton that is itself corrupted. The factor of two is a fixed anchor, not tuned to performance; under our corruptions $s$ saturates to $\{0,1\}$ (conditions that recompute the skeleton on degraded frames give $z_{\mathrm{skel}}\!\gg\!z_0$, those that reuse the clean skeleton cache give $z_{\mathrm{skel}}\!\approx\!z_0$), so its exact value does not affect the reported results. We scale the visual contribution by $w = 1-(1-w_{\mathrm{vl}})\,s$, applied to $p_{\mathrm{clip}}$ after its LayerNorm in both the gated and residual terms, so the fusion routes toward the corruption-robust skeleton exactly when the visual stream collapses and the skeleton remains clean. All statistics are pooled over the whole condition (transductive) and use no labels; the clean reference may be drawn from training or clean-test features.

Unpacking this compressed description, the mechanism consists of three scalars per corruption
condition, all computable from unlabeled data.

\textbf{The visual-reliability score $w_{\mathrm{vl}}$.} The solo scores
$s_{\mathrm{clip}}$ are obtained by running the trained fusion head with the skeleton stream
zeroed out: the skeleton projection receives a zero vector, so the resulting snippet scores
reflect the visual-language stream's discriminative ability alone. The standard deviation of
these scores over a corruption condition measures how well the stream still \emph{separates}
snippets---a discriminative signal, not a distributional one. This distinction is what
separates the method from statistic-restoration approaches: corruption is detected by its effect
on score separation, which is what the rank-based evaluation metric actually depends on, rather
than by its effect on feature moments, which may shift without harming (or harm without
shifting) the ranking. When corruption collapses the visual stream's score spread relative to
the clean reference, $w_{\mathrm{vl}}$ falls below one; when the stream is unaffected, the ratio
saturates at one and the mechanism stands down.

\textbf{The skeleton-reliability gate $s$.} Routing toward the skeleton is only safe if the
skeleton itself is clean. The gate compares the skeleton features' mean per-channel distribution
shift under the corruption condition, $z_{\mathrm{skel}}$, against the natural
clean-train-to-clean-test shift $z_0$ of the same features: shifts comparable to the natural
level ($z_{\mathrm{skel}} \approx z_0$) leave $s \approx 1$, while shifts at twice the natural
level or beyond drive $s$ to zero and disable rerouting entirely. The factor of two is a fixed
anchor chosen a priori, not tuned to performance; under the corruption benchmark of
Chapter~\ref{ch:04} the gate saturates to $\{0,1\}$---conditions that re-extract skeletons from
degraded frames (motion blur, JPEG) produce $z_{\mathrm{skel}} \gg z_0$ and are shut off, while
conditions that reuse the clean skeleton cache (Gaussian noise, brightness) sit at
$z_{\mathrm{skel}} \approx z_0$ and are enabled---so its exact value does not affect the
reported results.

\textbf{The routing scalar $w$.} The combined scalar $w = 1-(1-w_{\mathrm{vl}})\,s$ scales the
visual stream's projected features $p_{\mathrm{clip}}$, and its placement matters: the scaling
is applied \emph{after} the visual LayerNorm $\text{LN}_v$, in both the gated term and the
residual term of the gated fusion (Section~\ref{sec:meth-fusion})---scaling the input before the
LayerNorm would be nulled by the normalization itself, which re-centers and re-scales per
sample. The formula composes the two reliability estimates so that down-weighting occurs exactly
when the visual stream has collapsed ($w_{\mathrm{vl}} < 1$) \emph{and} the skeleton is trusted
($s > 0$); if either condition fails, $w = 1$ and the model is bit-identical to the unadapted
source model. The method can therefore only act where it is safe to act, and its failure mode is
to do nothing.

All statistics are pooled over the whole corruption condition---a transductive assumption,
standard in the corruption-robustness setting but disclosed as a limitation in
Chapter~\ref{ch:08}; a streaming variant that estimates $w$ causally is future work. The clean
reference statistics may be drawn from the training features or from clean test features, with
equivalent results (Chapter~\ref{ch:06}); no configuration of the method observes any label. The
mechanism is implemented in \texttt{src/tta/disc\_reweight.py} as pure helper functions (the
routing computation, the skeleton-zeroed forward pass, and the $w$-scaled forward pass), invoked
from the canonical evaluation entry point \texttt{src/tta/evaluate\_tta.py}.

In summary, the complete test-time procedure for one corruption condition is:
(1)~run the skeleton-zeroed forward pass over the condition's videos and compute the solo-score
dispersion $\sigma\big(s_{\mathrm{clip}}^{\mathrm{test}}\big)$, alongside the analogous clean
reference $\sigma\big(s_{\mathrm{clip}}^{\mathrm{clean}}\big)$;
(2)~compute the skeleton features' mean per-channel shift $z_{\mathrm{skel}}$ for the condition
and the clean reference shift $z_0$;
(3)~form $w_{\mathrm{vl}}$, $s$, and $w$ from the definitions above;
(4)~rescore the condition with the visual stream's projected features scaled by $w$ after
$\text{LN}_v$. A single scalar per condition is the method's entire adaptation state: no
gradient step, no optimizer, and no label is involved at any stage, and setting $w=1$ recovers
the source model exactly---the property that makes the method safe to leave permanently enabled.

Taken together, the three method families of this section span the design space that
Chapter~\ref{ch:06} evaluates: adapting normalization parameters
(Section~\ref{sec:meth-tta-entropy}), transforming features
(Section~\ref{sec:meth-tta-restore}), and re-routing modality trust (this section). Only the
third exploits the property that distinguishes this thesis's architecture from single-stream
detectors---the presence of a second, differently-corrupted modality behind a learned gate.
