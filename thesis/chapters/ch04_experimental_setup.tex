\chapter{Experimental Setup}
\label{ch:04}

This chapter specifies the experimental protocol under which every result in
Chapters~\ref{ch:05} and~\ref{ch:06} was produced: the two benchmark datasets
and their splits, the frame-level evaluation protocol, the construction of the
UCF-Crime-C corruption benchmark, the training configuration, the
reproducibility protocol, and the computational infrastructure. Throughout the
thesis we deliberately favor a fixed, fully-disclosed protocol over per-result
tuning: the same splits, the same seeds, and the same evaluation code are used
for every fusion variant and every backbone, so that cross-configuration
comparisons measure the configurations rather than the harness.

\section{Datasets}
\label{sec:datasets}

\subsection{UCF-Crime}

\textbf{UCF-Crime}~\cite{sultani2018ucfcrime} contains 1,900 untrimmed
surveillance videos spanning 13 anomaly categories (Abuse, Arrest, Arson,
Assault, Burglary, Explosion, Fighting, RoadAccidents, Robbery, Shooting,
Shoplifting, Stealing, Vandalism) plus a normal class. The training set
contains 800 normal and 810 anomalous videos with only video-level binary
labels---the weak-supervision setting that motivates the MIL formulation of
Chapter~\ref{ch:03}. The test set contains 150 normal and 140 anomalous videos
with frame-level temporal annotations, of which 254 meet the 64-frame minimum
and are evaluated. Videos are sourced from real surveillance cameras and
average approximately 4 minutes in length. The UCF-Crime frame
re-distribution used in this work provides $64\times64$-pixel PNG frames
pre-sampled at roughly 3~FPS (every tenth frame of the ${\sim}30$~FPS source
video); ``distributed as'' here refers to this frame re-distribution, not to
the original Sultani et al.\ video release. CLIP/SigLIP2 preprocessing
upsamples these frames to the backbone input size, and RTMPose operates on
them directly, so the input fidelity of every UCF-Crime experiment in this
thesis is lower than the native-resolution video consumed by published
pipelines. (XD-Violence, described next, is decoded from native video and is
unaffected.) The ${\sim}3$~FPS sampling also interacts with snippet duration
as discussed in Section~\ref{sec:protocol}.

\subsection{XD-Violence}

\textbf{XD-Violence}~\cite{wu2020xdviolence} is a larger multi-modal dataset
containing 4,754 untrimmed videos across six violence categories: Fighting
(B1), Shooting (B2), Riot (B4), Abuse (B5), Car Accident (B6), and Explosion
(G). Unlike UCF-Crime's fixed-camera surveillance footage, XD-Violence draws
from heterogeneous sources including movies, news broadcasts, sports events,
and online media, resulting in greater visual diversity, faster editing, and
more varied camera work. The training set contains 3,954 videos and the test
set contains 800 videos with frame-level annotations. XD-Violence is decoded
at its native ${\sim}24$~FPS. The two datasets therefore probe complementary
deployment regimes: UCF-Crime approximates a stationary CCTV installation,
while XD-Violence stresses generalization across production styles and scene
types.

\subsection{Data splits and model selection}

For both datasets, we hold out 15\% of the training set as a stratified
validation split, maintaining the normal/anomalous ratio across splits. The
split is generated once with a fixed seed and committed before the first
training run; every experiment in this thesis---all fusion variants, all four
backbones, all three training seeds---uses the identical split, so no
configuration can benefit from a more favorable validation partition.

Because the training videos carry only video-level labels, the validation
split is used for early stopping and per-run checkpoint selection via the
validation MIL loss; frame-level model selection is not possible on this
split. Frame-level test annotations are loaded exclusively inside the
evaluation entry point (\texttt{src/evaluate.py}), never by the training code,
so test-set information cannot leak into training or hyperparameter decisions.
Following the standard UCF-Crime and XD-Violence evaluation protocol, the
cross-backbone and ablation comparisons in Chapter~\ref{ch:05} report
frame-level test-set metrics.

\subsection{The 64-frame exclusion}

The skeleton stream segments each video into non-overlapping 64-frame
snippets (Chapter~\ref{ch:03}), which imposes a minimum video length. Videos
shorter than 64 frames (172 videos in UCF-Crime, one in XD-Violence) are
excluded from training and evaluation. On the UCF-Crime test set this leaves
the 254 evaluated videos noted above; on XD-Violence the exclusion affects a
single video. We disclose this exclusion prominently because it makes our
UCF-Crime evaluation cohort slightly smaller than the nominal 290-video test
set used by methods that operate on per-frame or 16-frame features; the
excluded videos are the shortest clips in the dataset (at the distributed
${\sim}3$~FPS frame rate, under ${\sim}21$ seconds of wall-clock time).

\section{Evaluation Protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}

\subsection{Metrics}

We evaluate using frame-level Area Under the ROC Curve (AUC) on UCF-Crime,
following the standard protocol established by Sultani et
al.~\cite{sultani2018ucfcrime}, and frame-level Average Precision (AP) on
XD-Violence, following its benchmark protocol~\cite{wu2020xdviolence}. Both
metrics are computed by pooling every frame of every test video into a single
ranking problem. AUC is insensitive to class imbalance and score calibration,
depending only on the relative ordering of anomalous versus normal frames; AP
weights precision across the recall range, depends on class prevalence, and
concentrates weight on the highest-ranked frames, making it more sensitive to
small ranking changes among top-scored frames---a distinction that matters for
the seed-sensitivity analysis in Chapter~\ref{ch:05}.

\subsection{Snippet-to-frame expansion}

The model scores 64-frame snippets, while the benchmarks are evaluated per
frame. Snippet-level anomaly scores are expanded to frame-level predictions by
assigning each frame the score of its enclosing 64-frame snippet. On
UCF-Crime, the final snippet's score is repeated to cover any trailing frames
of the full-length frame grid; on XD-Violence, evaluation is restricted to the
snippet-aligned portion (a whole number of 64-frame windows), so any trailing
partial window is dropped.

We disclose this protocol asymmetry explicitly. The UCF-Crime full-length
frame grid is reconstructed from a committed per-video frame-count manifest
(\texttt{data/\allowbreak ucf\_total\_frames.json}, covering all 1,900 videos), which
makes the reported UCF-Crime numbers reproducible from the repository alone;
XD-Violence uses the truncated snippet-aligned grid without a manifest. The
truncation on XD-Violence affects only trailing partial windows and is applied
identically to every configuration, so within-dataset comparisons are
unaffected; nevertheless, absolute numbers computed on the two grids are not
interchangeable, and we carry this caveat wherever it matters (in particular
for the corrupted-AUC tables of Chapter~\ref{ch:06}, which use the truncated
grid).

Because UCF-Crime is distributed at ${\sim}3$~FPS while XD-Violence is decoded
at its native ${\sim}24$~FPS, a 64-frame snippet spans about 20~s on UCF-Crime
versus ${\sim}2.7$~s on XD-Violence; both streams use identical snippet
boundaries, so the skeleton and visual features stay aligned by construction.

\section{The UCF-Crime-C Corruption Benchmark}
\label{sec:ucfcrimec}

Chapter~\ref{ch:06} studies deployment robustness under input corruption. No
corruption benchmark existed for UCF-Crime, so we constructed
\textbf{UCF-Crime-C} by applying four corruption types at five severity levels
to the UCF-Crime test set, yielding 20 corruption conditions in total. The
corruption families and their severity parameterizations follow the ImageNet-C
protocol of Hendrycks and Dietterich~\cite{hendrycks2019imagenetc}: Gaussian
noise (sensor noise from low-light cameras), motion blur (camera shake or
fast-moving objects), JPEG compression (bandwidth-limited transmission), and
brightness shift (illumination change). These four simulate the distribution
shifts most plausible for surveillance deployment while remaining exactly
reproducible.

\subsection{Severity parameterization}

Table~\ref{tab:corruption-params} lists the exact severity parameters as
implemented in the tracked corruption module
(\texttt{scripts/corruption.py}); these match the verified ImageNet-C
reference values. Gaussian noise adds zero-mean noise with standard deviation
$\sigma$ to the image normalized to $[0,1]$. JPEG compression runs an
encode--decode cycle at the stated quality factor (lower is worse). Brightness
shift is an \emph{additive} offset applied to the normalized $[0,1]$ image
(not a multiplicative gain), followed by clipping. Motion blur convolves with
a directional Gaussian kernel parameterized by kernel size and $\sigma$. All
four transforms are implemented with \texttt{numpy} and \texttt{cv2} only, so
the corruption pipeline is bit-identical across the extraction environments of
Section~\ref{sec:infra}, and each transform is driven by a seeded random
generator. A visual perceptual check on real UCF-Crime frames confirmed that
each corruption type at severity 3 and above is clearly distinguishable from
the clean frame.

\subsection{Feature re-extraction policy}
\label{sec:reextraction}

Corrupting the test set invalidates cached features, so features must be
re-extracted from the corrupted frames. Visual-language features are
re-extracted for all 20 conditions and all four backbones: the frozen VLM
encoder sees the corrupted pixels directly, which is precisely the
degradation pathway the robustness study targets.

For the skeleton stream we made a measured cost decision. Skeleton features
are re-extracted only for motion blur and JPEG compression
(\texttt{SKELETON\_REEXTRACT\_TYPES} in \texttt{scripts/corruption.py}); for
Gaussian noise and brightness shift, RTMPose produces effectively identical
keypoints on the corrupted frames, so the clean skeleton cache is reused
unchanged. This reflects the empirical robustness of top-down pose estimation
to photometric perturbations---detection and keypoint regression survive
additive noise and brightness offsets that severely disturb dense VLM
embeddings---while spatially destructive corruptions (blur smearing limb
boundaries, JPEG block artifacts) do change the extracted poses and therefore
require re-extraction. Skeleton re-extraction is the most expensive stage of
the pipeline (Section~\ref{sec:efficiency}), so restricting it to the two
corruption families that need it kept the benchmark construction tractable on
a single workstation. This asymmetry is not merely an engineering footnote: it
creates the clean-skeleton/corrupted-visual asymmetry that the test-time
reweighting mechanism of Chapter~\ref{ch:06} exploits, and we return to it
there.

\section{Implementation Details}
\label{sec:impl}

All experiments are conducted on a single NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPU (24~GB).
Skeleton features are extracted using RTMPose-m~\cite{jiang2023rtmpose} with
YOLOX-m detection at approximately 21.6 FPS, followed by
CTR-GCN~\cite{chen2021ctrgcn} encoding with NTU-120 pre-trained
weights~\cite{yan2022pyskl} across all four streams (joint, bone, joint
motion, bone motion). Visual features are extracted at 1~FPS with the
respective frozen backbone.

The fusion head and anomaly classifier are trained using AdamW (weight decay
$10^{-2}$) with a learning rate of $1 \times 10^{-4}$ for both datasets. The
learning rate follows a linear warmup over the first 5 epochs, then
cosine-annealing to zero. We use a batch size of 16 normal/anomalous pairs (32
videos per batch) and train for a maximum of 50 epochs with early stopping
(patience of 10 epochs on validation loss). Each video is represented as a
fixed-length bag of $T=32$ snippets (uniform segment sampling when longer,
sampling with replacement when shorter). The top-$k$ selection in MIL ranking
uses $k=3$ for both datasets. We use a fixed hyperparameter configuration
across both datasets (learning rate $1 \times 10^{-4}$, $k=3$, margin $1.0$)
and a sparsity weight $\lambda_1 = 8 \times 10^{-3}$; the sensitivity of the
headline results to this fixed configuration is analyzed in
Chapter~\ref{ch:05}. The temporal smoothness weight $\lambda_2$ in the total
training loss (Chapter~\ref{ch:03}) is the one dataset-dependent setting: we
use $\lambda_2 = 8 \times 10^{-4}$ on UCF-Crime but $\lambda_2 = 0$ on
XD-Violence. We found that penalizing adjacent-snippet score differences
over-regularizes and destabilizes the AP metric on XD-Violence's
heterogeneous, rapidly-cut sources (movies, news, sports), while providing a
small benefit on UCF-Crime's continuous fixed-camera
footage.\footnote{Training provenance for the UCF-Crime numbers: an earlier
implementation of the smoothness term penalized score differences across the
batch axis (adjacent videos within a training batch) rather than across
temporally adjacent snippets; the released code applies the penalty strictly
along the temporal (adjacent-snippet) axis. The earlier implementation is
recoverable from the repository history (original at commit \texttt{e5ab412},
corrected in \texttt{f89910b}) and its formula is documented in
\texttt{tests/test\_smoothness\_axis.py}. All seed-42 UCF-Crime runs and all
three seeds of the Gated Fusion rows (including the 82.5$\pm$0.4 headline)
were trained with the earlier implementation; seeds 123 and 2024 of the
remaining UCF-Crime table rows were trained after the correction with the
released temporal implementation at the identical
$\lambda_2 = 8 \times 10^{-4}$. Those rows therefore combine two loss
implementations across their seeds, and their standard deviations should not
be read as pure seed variance. A direct A/B comparison on the headline
configuration (seed 42) measured a $-0.13$ percentage-point AUC shift from
the correction. The UCF-Crime results are reported as run. A post-submission
consistency rerun retrained all twenty-nine legacy-implementation cells with
the released code under a version-locked clean working tree: the headline
reproduces at 82.5\% ($-0.06$ points, with all three headline seeds inside
their seed band) and every qualitative conclusion of Chapter~\ref{ch:05} is
preserved; per-cell deltas are tracked in the released repository
(\texttt{results/\_consistency\_rerun/}).}

For all fusion variants we report mean and standard deviation over three
random seeds (42, 123, 2024) to quantify training stability. The seed controls
weight initialization, batch composition, and snippet sampling; feature
extraction is deterministic and shared across seeds.

\section{Reproducibility Protocol}
\label{sec:repro}

Every experiment in this thesis is designed to be re-runnable and auditable:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Fixed seeds and splits.} All runs use the declared seeds
    \{42, 123, 2024\} and the single committed 15\% stratified validation
    split. No experiment was selected from a larger undisclosed pool.
    \item \textbf{Per-run configuration snapshots.} Each run directory
    contains a \texttt{config\_\allowbreak snapshot.json} capturing the exact
    configuration the run executed with, so a result can always be traced to
    its full hyperparameter set rather than to a config file that may have
    changed since.
    \item \textbf{Bit-identical re-run invariant.} Re-executing a training
    configuration with the same seed reproduces the stored metrics; this
    invariant was verified during development and is the basis for treating
    single-run metrics as deterministic facts rather than samples.
    \item \textbf{Master results index.} Every completed run appends a row to
    \texttt{results/\allowbreak results-index.csv} (over 1,000 runs across the project),
    which serves as the master index from which the sweep and TTA analyses of
    Chapters~\ref{ch:05} and~\ref{ch:06} are derived.
    \item \textbf{Evaluation manifest.} The UCF-Crime full-length frame grid
    is reconstructed from the committed manifest
    \texttt{data/ucf\_total\_frames.json} (Section~\ref{sec:protocol}), so the
    headline UCF-Crime numbers are reproducible from the repository without
    access to the raw videos.
\end{itemize}

The repository provides a complete reproduction path, from
environment setup through feature extraction, training, evaluation, and
regeneration of every table and figure in this thesis.

\section{Computational Infrastructure}
\label{sec:infra}

The three-stage pipeline imposes mutually incompatible software requirements,
which we resolved with three isolated conda environments on a single Windows
11 workstation:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{vcc-skeleton} (Python 3.11): RTMPose-m + YOLOX-m skeleton
    extraction via \texttt{rtmlib} and \texttt{onnxruntime-gpu} (CUDA 12).
    Using the ONNX runtime avoids the full \texttt{mmpose}/\texttt{mmcv}
    stack entirely for the pose stage.
    \item \textbf{vcc-ctrgcn} (Python 3.10): the CTR-GCN forward pass through
    PYSKL, which pins \texttt{mmcv-full}~1.7.0---a library that only compiles
    against PyTorch 1.x. This environment is therefore a legacy silo running
    PyTorch 1.12.1 (CUDA 11.3) solely for frozen skeleton encoding.
    \item \textbf{vcc-main} (Python 3.11): everything else---CLIP/SigLIP2
    feature extraction via \texttt{open-clip-torch}, MIL training, evaluation,
    TTA, and figure generation---on PyTorch 2.6 (CUDA 12.4).
\end{itemize}

The split is forced by a hard incompatibility: PYSKL requires
\texttt{mmcv-full}~1.x, which cannot coexist with the PyTorch 2.x that the
modern VLM stack requires. Because all backbones are frozen, the environments
communicate only through cached feature files on disk (one \texttt{.npy} per
video per stream), which also guarantees that every training run across
Chapters~\ref{ch:05} and~\ref{ch:06} consumes byte-identical inputs.

Three observations frame the results chapters. First, the trainable component
is tiny: across the four visual backbones the gated fusion head spans
0.50M--1.02M trainable parameters (scaling with the visual feature dimension),
which is what makes the full $6 \times 4 \times 3$
variant-backbone-seed grid of Chapter~\ref{ch:05} feasible---training
completes in under 5 minutes per configuration on the single RTX 4090.
Second, the run-time cost of the system is dominated not by the trainable head
(sub-0.1~ms per snippet) but by frozen feature extraction, and within
extraction by the skeleton stage: pose estimation costs ${\sim}30$~ms per
frame in isolation (Table~\ref{tab:efficiency-backbones}), and the full
extraction loop sustains about 21.6~FPS on single-person scenes
(Chapter~\ref{ch:03}), 99.3\% of that time being model inference---an order
of magnitude slower per frame than CLIP embedding. Third, the gated fusion head
is \emph{smaller} than the late-fusion head (498K vs 633K parameters in the
CLIP configuration) while performing substantially better
(Chapter~\ref{ch:05}), because it shares a single fused scorer instead of
duplicating per-modality classifier heads.
