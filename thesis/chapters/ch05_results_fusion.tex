\chapter{Results: Fusion and Backbone Study}
\label{ch:05}

This chapter presents the main experimental results of the thesis: the
six-variant ablation across four visual-language backbones on both benchmarks
(Section~\ref{sec:ablation}), the backbone comparison and its
dataset-dependent preferences (Section~\ref{sec:backbones}), the
skeleton-complementarity analysis (Section~\ref{sec:complementarity}), a
hyperparameter-sensitivity study conducted in an earlier historical
configuration (Section~\ref{sec:sweep}), the statistical robustness of the
headline numbers (Sections~\ref{sec:bootstrap} and~\ref{sec:seeds}),
qualitative behavior (Section~\ref{sec:qualitative}), a failure-case analysis
(Section~\ref{sec:failure}), and a per-category summary
(Section~\ref{sec:percat}). All ablation numbers in this chapter are 3-seed
means with standard deviations over seeds \{42, 123, 2024\}, under the
protocol of Chapter~\ref{ch:04}.

The headline results of the thesis are read directly from these tables: on
UCF-Crime, Gated Fusion with the SigLIP2 Giant backbone reaches
\textbf{82.5$\pm$0.4\% AUC}; on XD-Violence, Gated Fusion with the SigLIP2
SO400M backbone reaches \textbf{78.7$\pm$0.9\% AP}. The remainder of this
section walks through the three findings the tables establish.

\textbf{Skeleton features provide complementary information.} The
skeleton-only baseline achieves 68.8\% AUC on UCF-Crime and 40.8\% AP on
XD-Violence---substantially below the visual-only baselines. However, this
lower standalone performance does not diminish the skeleton modality's
contribution to fusion. Gated fusion meets or exceeds the visual-only baseline
in all eight backbone$\times$dataset configurations (mean $+0.6$ points;
strictly positive in six of eight, the other two tied at the displayed
precision, so a tie-dropping sign test over these eight---correlated, since
they share one frozen skeleton stream---configurations gives $p\approx0.03$)
and never degrades a strong visual backbone. The per-configuration gains are
modest and individually within seed variance, but their consistency indicates
that the skeleton stream supplies a small, reliable signal not captured by
visual appearance alone---most pronounced on XD-Violence (up to $+2.1$ AP with
SigLIP2 SO400M). Section~\ref{sec:complementarity} examines this argument in
detail.

\textbf{Gated fusion outperforms late fusion consistently.} The gated fusion
mechanism outperforms simple late fusion (equal-weight score averaging) on
every backbone on \emph{both} datasets: the UCF-Crime margins are $+2.5$,
$+1.8$, $+2.2$, and $+2.6$ points and the XD-Violence margins are $+12.6$,
$+10.9$, $+13.0$, and $+11.1$ points (CLIP, SigLIP2 Base, SO400M, Giant).
This gap is particularly notable on
XD-Violence, where late fusion with CLIP achieves only 63.9\% AP compared to
76.5\% for gated fusion---a difference of 12.6 percentage points. We attribute
this to the learned gating mechanism's ability to weight modalities
differently for each input, reducing the weaker modality's relative
contribution when its signal is noisy. On XD-Violence's heterogeneous sources,
snippets vary widely in how informative the skeleton stream is (crowd scenes
versus close-up interpersonal violence versus vehicle accidents), so a fixed
equal weight is a particularly poor fit; the per-dimension gate of
Chapter~\ref{ch:03} can suppress the skeleton contribution on
appearance-driven snippets while retaining it where articulated human motion
carries the event.

\textbf{Late fusion can degrade performance.} A notable finding is that late
fusion sometimes performs worse than the visual-only baseline. On UCF-Crime,
CLIP late fusion (78.9\%) falls below CLIP visual-only (81.2\%), a drop of 2.3
percentage points. This suggests that the fixed equal weighting lets the
weaker skeleton signal drag down every fused score, with no input-dependent
mechanism to reduce its weight relative to the stronger visual stream. The
gated mechanism resolves this by learning to modulate each modality's
contribution: it recovers everything late fusion loses and adds a margin on
top, on both datasets and all four backbones. The practical lesson is that
\emph{how} modalities are combined matters at least as much as \emph{whether}
a second modality is added---an unweighted combination of unequal streams is
worse than not fusing at all.

\subsection{Pooling ablations}

The GF 2-Person and GF Mean-Only rows probe the two aggregation choices fixed
in Chapter~\ref{ch:03}. The picture differs by dataset. On UCF-Crime the three
gated variants are statistically indistinguishable at the headline backbone
(82.5$\pm$0.4, 82.6$\pm$0.3, and 82.5$\pm$0.4 for default, 2-Person, and
Mean-Only with SigLIP2 Giant): pooling choices are not where UCF-Crime
performance lives. On XD-Violence the default aggregation wins on average:
across the four backbones the 2-person skeleton aggregation costs about
0.90~AP relative to default gated fusion (it helps only SO400M, and hurts
CLIP by 2.1~AP), and mean-only visual pooling is consistently negative
(between $-0.3$ and $-0.5$ AP on every backbone). The original pooling study
that fixed these defaults---a single-configuration comparison on the CLIP
backbone conducted before the final training recipe---measured the same
ordering with larger margins ($-0.90$~AP for 2-person concatenation and
$-2.32$~AP for mean-only pooling), and the final 3-seed grid confirms its
direction on every backbone.

Two cells in the 2-Person row are nominally the highest values in their
tables: 82.6$\pm$0.3 on UCF-Crime (Giant) and 79.2$\pm$0.3 on XD-Violence
(SO400M). We deliberately do \emph{not} promote these as headline results.
The differences over default gated fusion ($+0.1$ and $+0.5$ points) are
within or near seed variance, the 2-person aggregation is \emph{worse} than
the default for the majority of backbone$\times$dataset cells, and selecting
the per-cell maximum across ablation variants after seeing the results would
constitute a mild form of test-set selection. The headline configuration is
the default-pooling Gated Fusion row, fixed before the backbone study was
run.

A key finding of our study is that \emph{backbone choice interacts with
dataset characteristics} in ways that are not predicted by general-purpose
benchmark rankings. Figure~\ref{fig:backbone-comparison} visualizes these
interactions across all variants.

\textbf{UCF-Crime favors larger backbones.} On UCF-Crime, SigLIP2 Giant
consistently achieves the highest scores across nearly all fusion variants:
82.4\% visual-only, 82.5\% gated fusion, and 82.5\% with mean-only pooling.
The ranking follows model capacity---Giant $>$ SO400M $\approx$ CLIP $>$
SigLIP2 Base---suggesting that the 13-category surveillance setting benefits
from richer visual representations. The capacity ordering also has a clear
loser: SigLIP2 Base is the weakest backbone on UCF-Crime in every variant,
trailing CLIP ViT-B/16 by 2.7 points visual-only (78.5 vs 81.2) and by 2.4
points under gated fusion (79.0 vs 81.4). This is itself informative: SigLIP2
Base and CLIP ViT-B/16 are architecturally comparable ViT-B/16 encoders, so
the gap indicates that training objective and data recipe alone do not
transfer uniformly to surveillance footage---SigLIP2's advantages at the Base
scale do not materialize on UCF-Crime, while its larger variants do pay off.

\textbf{XD-Violence favors SO400M under fusion.} On XD-Violence the four
backbones' visual-only AP is statistically indistinguishable across seeds
(CLIP $74.6$, SigLIP2 Base $74.5$, SO400M $76.6$, Giant $76.8$; std
$1.0$--$2.2$), so visual-only capacity does not cleanly rank them. Under gated
fusion, however, SO400M emerges as the clear and stable leader ($78.7$\% AP),
ahead of Giant ($76.8$\%). This may reflect SO400M's shape-optimized
architecture providing a better capacity fit for XD-Violence's heterogeneous
content (movies, sports, and online media) than for the fixed-camera
surveillance of UCF-Crime.

\textbf{The skeleton's benefit is backbone-dependent.} Because the visual-only
XD-Violence scores fall within seed variance, they do not reliably rank the
backbones; gated fusion is more stable (std $0.9$\% AP for three of the four)
and resolves a clear leader (SO400M, $78.7$\%) that the visual-only numbers do
not single out. The gain that fusion adds over the visual-only baseline also
varies with the backbone---largest for CLIP and SO400M ($+1.9$ and $+2.1$ AP)
and negligible for SigLIP2 Base and Giant---so the skeleton stream's
complementary value depends on the visual representation it is fused with
rather than on raw visual capacity alone, a dependence that single-backbone
evaluations would miss. This is a methodological point as much as an empirical
one: had this study evaluated only the original CLIP backbone (as most
frozen-feature VAD work does), the complementarity finding would have looked
stronger than it generalizes to be; had it evaluated only SigLIP2 Giant on
XD-Violence, fusion would have looked like a null result.

\section{Complementarity Analysis}
\label{sec:complementarity}

The complementarity claim---that a 68.8\%-AUC skeleton stream improves
fusion with 81--82\%-AUC visual streams---deserves scrutiny, because it would
be easy to overstate. We state it in its defensible form: the gain is
\emph{small but consistent}.

The evidence for consistency is the 8-of-8 pattern in
Tables~\ref{tab:ucf-ablation} and~\ref{tab:xd-ablation}: gated fusion meets or
exceeds its visual-only counterpart for every backbone on both datasets. Six
of the eight configurations are strictly positive; the remaining two (SigLIP2
Base and Giant on XD-Violence) are ties at the displayed precision. Treating
each configuration as one paired observation and dropping the ties, a sign
test gives $p\approx0.03$---with the caveat, disclosed in our workshop
paper~\cite{jeng2026dualmodal} and repeated here, that the eight configurations
are correlated because they share
a single frozen skeleton stream, so this is a consistency check rather than a
claim of eight independent replications. The evidence for smallness is equally
clear: the mean gain is $+0.6$ points, no individual configuration's gain
exceeds its own seed std by a large factor, and the largest gains ($+1.9$ AP
for CLIP, $+2.1$ AP for SO400M, both on XD-Violence) remain modest.

Mechanistically, the skeleton stream contributes motion dynamics that
appearance-based VLM features do not encode: articulated body configuration
and its first-order temporal evolution (Chapter~\ref{ch:03}). That the largest
gains occur on XD-Violence---where events like fighting are defined by body
motion rather than scene context---is consistent with this reading, as is the
gated mechanism's large advantage over late fusion (which shows the network
extracts usable signal from the skeleton stream when allowed to weight it).
A per-class breakdown of where fusion helps and hurts, based on grouping
UCF-Crime categories into human-motion-centric and appearance-centric classes,
is deferred to Appendix~\ref{app:b}; its verdict is directionally supportive
but mixed and noisy at per-class sample sizes, and we do not lean on it for
the headline claim.

\section{Hyperparameter Sensitivity (Historical Configuration)}
\label{sec:sweep}

Before the backbone study, we ran a staged learning-rate $\times$ top-$k$
sweep to test whether the fixed training configuration of
Chapter~\ref{ch:04} was leaving performance on the table. We report it here
for completeness and because its outcome differs sharply between datasets.

\textbf{This is a historical-configuration study.} The sweep was conducted in
the 2026-05 configuration of the system: CLIP ViT-B/16 backbone only, seed 42
for the grid, and---critically---\emph{before} the adoption of $\lambda_2 = 0$
on XD-Violence (Chapter~\ref{ch:04}). Its baselines (82.27\% AUC on UCF-Crime,
71.92\% AP on XD-Violence, single-seed) are therefore sweep-era reference
points, and none of its numbers are comparable to, or chain into, the headline
results of Section~\ref{sec:ablation}, which use a different smoothness
setting, multiple backbones, and 3-seed reporting. The value of the sweep is
its \emph{shape}, which is a property of the loss landscape rather than of the
exact configuration.

The two sweep heatmaps (99 configurations per dataset, seed 42, with 3-seed
confirmation runs at the winning cells) appear at full scale in
Appendix~\ref{app:c} as Figures~\ref{fig:appc-sweep-ucf}
and~\ref{fig:appc-sweep-xd}. The two datasets answer the tuning question in
opposite ways:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{XD-Violence benefits from tuning.} The sweep winner
    ($\mathrm{lr}=10^{-3}$, $k=2$) reached 74.69\% AP in its 3-seed
    confirmation, $+3.72$ points over the sweep-era 3-seed baseline of
    70.98\% AP---a substantial improvement \emph{within the historical
    configuration}. Higher learning rates with small $k$ dominate,
    suggesting the default $\mathrm{lr}=10^{-4}$ under-trains the head on
    XD-Violence's larger training set.
    \item \textbf{UCF-Crime is hyperparameter-insensitive.} The best cell
    ($\mathrm{lr}=1.5\times10^{-3}$, $k=9$) confirmed at 82.12\% AUC,
    $+0.14$ points over its 81.98\% AUC sweep-era 3-seed baseline---within
    noise. The UCF-Crime surface is flat: almost any reasonable
    (lr, $k$) cell lands within a point of the default.
\end{itemize}

We deliberately did \emph{not} fold the XD-Violence sweep winner into the
final configuration: the headline results keep the fixed cross-dataset
hyperparameters of Chapter~\ref{ch:04} for comparability across the
$6\times4$ variant--backbone grid, and the later adoption of $\lambda_2=0$ on
XD-Violence---a change the sweep predates---already recovered the bulk of the
XD-Violence headroom through a different mechanism. The sweep's enduring
lessons are that XD-Violence is the tunable dataset of the pair and that
UCF-Crime results are robust to reasonable hyperparameter choices, which
derisks the fixed-configuration comparisons elsewhere in this thesis. Full
grids, ranked tables, and confirmation details appear in
Appendix~\ref{app:c}.

\section{Statistical Robustness of the Headline Numbers}
\label{sec:bootstrap}

% Source: results/_analysis_2026-06-10/pri6_bootstrap_ci.{json,md} (tracked).
% Video-level bootstrap, B=2000, rng seed 12345, s42 checkpoints; pooled
% recompute matched stored eval_metrics exactly before resampling.
Seed variance measures training stability, but it does not measure the
uncertainty induced by the finite test set. To quantify the latter we
bootstrap the evaluation cohort at the \emph{video} level: resample test
videos with replacement ($B=2000$, fixed RNG seed 12345), pool the frames of
each resample, and recompute the metric. Videos---not frames---are the
exchangeable unit, since frames within a video are strongly dependent. The
analysis uses the seed-42 checkpoint of each headline configuration, and the
pooled recomputation was first verified to match the stored evaluation
metrics exactly.

The resulting 95\% confidence intervals are wide:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{UCF-Crime} (seed-42 point estimate 82.49\% AUC): CI
    $[75.63, 88.02]$, width 12.39 percentage points.
    \item \textbf{XD-Violence} (seed-42 point estimate 79.74\% AP): CI
    $[76.62, 82.68]$, width 6.06 percentage points.
\end{itemize}

Two readings follow. First, the UCF-Crime interval is strikingly wide
relative to the differences typically celebrated in the VAD literature: its
width exceeds the spread of the entire published leaderboard on this
benchmark. This is a property of the benchmark, not of our method---the
evaluated UCF-Crime cohort contains roughly 250 videos, fewer than 130 of
them anomalous, and any method reporting a single frame-level AUC on this
test set inherits video-level resampling uncertainty of this order.
Cross-method gaps of one or two points on UCF-Crime should accordingly be
read with restraint, including where they favor us. Second, the XD-Violence
interval is half as wide, reflecting its larger 800-video test set;
conclusions drawn on XD-Violence rest on firmer statistical ground, which is
one reason this thesis emphasizes that its competitiveness is clearest on
XD-Violence AP. These intervals are computed for the seed-42 checkpoints
only; combining test-set resampling with seed variance would widen them
further.

\section{Seed Sensitivity}
\label{sec:seeds}

% Source: std columns of Tables 1-2 above (paper/main.tex canonical tables).
On UCF-Crime, Gated Fusion exhibits low variance across seeds for all
backbones (standard deviation 0.1--0.4\% AUC), reflecting stable training
dynamics on this dataset. On XD-Violence, three of the four backbones are
comparably stable---CLIP, SigLIP2 Base, and SO400M all sit near 0.9\% AP
standard deviation---while SigLIP2 Giant is the lone outlier at 2.8\%. The
visual-only variants are noisier still on XD-Violence (std 1.0--2.2\% AP),
which is precisely why Section~\ref{sec:backbones} declines to rank backbones
from visual-only numbers.

This stability pattern is tied to the dataset-dependent smoothness setting
(Chapter~\ref{ch:04}): disabling the temporal smoothness term on XD-Violence
not only improves AP but also markedly tightens seed variance, indicating that
the elevated variance seen in smoothness-regularized configurations is in
part a regularization artifact rather than an intrinsic property of the
dataset. The residual sensitivity of SigLIP2 Giant likely reflects (1)~AP's
inherent sensitivity to score calibration relative to AUC, and
(2)~XD-Violence's category imbalance and heterogeneous sources creating a
more complex optimization landscape for the highest-capacity backbone. The
practical consequence for experimental methodology is stated once here and
observed throughout the thesis: on XD-Violence, no comparison is made between
a 3-seed metric and a single-seed metric, because single-seed XD-Violence AP
differences of up to two points routinely vanish under reseeding.

\section{Failure Analysis}
\label{sec:failure}

% Source: results/_analysis_2026-06-10/pri9_failure_cases.md (tracked);
% run ucf_gated_fusion_giant_s42; pooled frame AUC reconstruction matched the
% stored value exactly before per-video analysis. Video-level AUC figures are
% deliberately NOT cited (known recompute discrepancy, deferred).
To understand what the headline UCF-Crime model gets wrong, we analyzed the
per-video behavior of the seed-42 SigLIP2 Giant gated-fusion checkpoint. The
evaluated cohort contains 254 test videos (128 with at least one annotated
anomalous frame, 126 fully normal), totaling 82{,}930 anomalous and
1{,}014{,}120 normal frames (7.6\% positive). The pooled frame-level AUC
reconstruction was verified to match the stored metric exactly before any
per-video statistics were computed.

\textbf{Failures fall into two regimes.} Table~\ref{tab:failure-cases} lists
the five worst-ranked anomalous videos. The first regime is
\emph{saturated-high near-ties}: in videos like Burglary017 and Shooting028
the model scores essentially every frame near the ceiling (anomalous and
normal frame means both ${\approx}0.99$), so the within-video ranking is
decided by noise and can invert completely. The anomaly signal here is
scene-level rather than event-level---the model has decided the whole video
looks anomalous and cannot localize within it. The second regime is
\emph{short-event misses}: RoadAccidents011 contains an event spanning only
1.9\% of its frames, and the model scores the entire video near 0.000---a
64-frame snippet-pooled head simply dilutes an event much shorter than its
temporal receptive field. Explosion007 (4.2\% anomalous) shows the same
pattern. Both regimes align with category-level weaknesses: abrupt,
visually-transient events (RoadAccidents, Explosion, Shooting) are exactly
the categories where snippet pooling costs the most
(Section~\ref{sec:percat}).

\textbf{False positives are sustained, not transient.} The five
highest-scoring normal videos (Normal\_Videos\_887, 925, 884, 895, 894) are
not videos with a single spurious peak: each sustains near-ceiling scores
across 92--100\% of its frames. These are scene-wide false alarms---normal
footage whose appearance (crowds, dense motion) resembles anomalous context
throughout. Under a deployment threshold these videos would alarm
continuously, which is a more operationally damaging error mode than isolated
blips.

% (both tracked; giant-s42 provenance).
\begin{figure}[t]

  \caption{Frame-score distributions for anomalous versus normal frames
  (UCF-Crime, Gated Fusion SigLIP2 Giant, seed 42). Both distributions are

\textbf{The model separates on average but is poorly calibrated.} Anomalous
frames average 0.874 versus 0.382 for normal frames---a healthy mean
separation of $+0.49$---but the normal distribution is heavy-tailed upward:
its 75th percentile is 0.986 (Figure~\ref{fig:score-hist}). In other words,
a quarter of all normal frames receive near-certain anomaly scores. AUC, being
rank-based, absorbs this as long as anomalous frames rank higher still, but
any fixed-threshold deployment would face a severe false-alarm rate. Score
calibration---not ranking---is the binding limitation of the current head,
and we return to it in Section~\ref{sec:lim-calibration}.

\section{Per-Category Performance}
\label{sec:percat}

% Source: thesis/tables/tab_percat_{ucf,xd}.tex (Class R, generated by
% scripts/thesis_per_category_tables.py from tracked
% results/{ucf_gated_fusion_giant,xd_gated_fusion_so400m}_s{42,123,2024}/
% per_category.csv, post-lambda0/post-h1). Values quoted below match those
% generated tables; the full tables are rendered in Appendix B.
Finally, we summarize per-category behavior of the two headline
configurations, computed 3-seed from the current canonical run directories;
the complete tables appear in Appendix~\ref{app:b}.

On \textbf{UCF-Crime} (Gated Fusion, SigLIP2 Giant), the strongest categories
are Stealing (99.34$\pm$0.13\% AUC), Vandalism (97.47$\pm$0.38), and Assault
(97.18$\pm$0.45); the weakest are RoadAccidents (87.14$\pm$0.58), Robbery
(88.64$\pm$0.71), and Explosion (88.75$\pm$1.04). The weak tail is exactly the
abrupt, short-event category set identified by the failure analysis
(Section~\ref{sec:failure}): categories whose events are brief and visually
transient lose the most to 64-frame snippet pooling, while categories with
sustained, appearance-distinctive activity approach ceiling.

On \textbf{XD-Violence} (Gated Fusion, SigLIP2 SO400M), the spread is far
wider: Riot reaches 94.03$\pm$0.27\% AP and Fighting 81.13$\pm$0.23, while
Abuse (43.57$\pm$3.53) and Car Accident (48.16$\pm$1.18) fall below 50\%, with
Shooting at 56.84$\pm$0.95 and Explosion at 72.22$\pm$1.11 between. The
ordering is interpretable: Riot and Fighting are crowd- and body-motion-driven
events that both streams detect; Abuse is subtle, close-quarters interpersonal
violence with little distinctive full-body motion or scene signature; Car
Accident and Shooting hinge on brief, small-scale visual moments. Note that
per-category values are not directly comparable to the pooled headline
metrics, which rank all frames of all categories jointly and therefore also
pay for cross-video calibration errors that per-category evaluation does not
expose.
